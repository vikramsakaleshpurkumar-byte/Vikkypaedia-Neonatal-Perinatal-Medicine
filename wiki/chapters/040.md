40 Care of Periviable Micropreemies: The Japanese Perspective

### SATOSHI KUSUDA, KENICHI MASUMOTO, AND HIDEHIKO NAKANISHI

### Introduction

One of the most rapidly changing areas of perinatal care over the last several decades is the care of preterm infants at a limit of viability, defined as preterm infants born before 24 weeks of gestation.1 Only limited centers or countries have been providing comprehensive and intensive treatment for these infants due to poor outcomes. On the other hand, the Japanese academia involved in perinatal medicine has been attempting to treat and care for these periviable preterm infants for almost the last 25 years.2 Because of this effort, the overall outcomes of these infants have been improving constantly. In order to achieve progress in the care of periviable preterm infants, aggressive intervention is the first priority. Then, the accumulation of experiences and the sharing of these experiences become crucial. Many practices applied to periviable preterm infants have not been established through well-designed clinical trials because most clinical trials have excluded periviable preterm infants. Sharing information is important for further improvement in the care of periviable preterm infants. Therefore, in this chapter, Japanese experiences and challenges in the care of periviable preterm infants are introduced, especially with regard to care in the acute phase.3

### Background

After 1990, when the Maternal Health Act lowered the upper limit of interventional abortion from 24 to 22 weeks of pregnancy, some perinatal centers in Japan started to resuscitate infants born before 24 weeks of gestation. Of course, before the amendment, several periviable preterm infants who survived to discharge in limited perinatal centers were

reported, and these actual real-world data became a catalyst for the amendment.4 Since then, an increased number of survivors has been reported, and the outcomes of periviable infants have gradually been improving. Although there are still many challenges in the care of periviable preterm infants at present, the practices adopted at different clinical sites should be shared and through these processes, standardized care based on scientific knowledge can be utilized to address these challenges on a global level. Furthermore, sharing information is also crucial to ensure collaboration among several stakeholders.5

### Outcomes

The neonatal database operated by the Neonatal Research Network of Japan has been collecting outcomes among very preterm infants since 2003 to evaluate the current situation in the care of preterm infants and to improve their outcomes through quality improvement.6 According to this database, the most recent survival rates in infants born at 22 and 23 weeks of gestation are around 60% and 80%, respectively (Fig. 40.1).7 Mortality rates are higher compared to those of infants with a higher gestational age; however, an overall improvement can be seen. Furthermore, the incidence of cerebral palsy at 3 years of age among survivors has not been increasing but rather decreasing (Fig. 40.2).7 Conversely, the high incidence of developmental delay among survivors, which ranges between 50% and 60%, is becoming an important issue in periviable preterm infants, and it remains at a critical level.8–10 These results mean that the intensive care of periviable preterm infants can improve their survival rates without increasing the incidence of neurological deficits. ### Antenatal Corticosteroid Use

Table 40.1 shows the international guidelines for antenatal glucocorticoid use for preterm delivery.11–15 Most guidelines recommend the routine use of antenatal glucocorticoids after 24 weeks of pregnancy, with consideration at 22 and 23 weeks of gestation. However, if the recent improvement in the survival rate of periviable preterm infants is considered, the antenatal corticosteroid should be used after 22 weeks of pregnancy unless consent is not obtained.16

### Selection of Delivery Mode

A meta-analysis published in 2020 showed that a cesarean delivery for singleton preterm infants born before 28 weeks of gestation significantly reduced mortality; however, it could not show any beneficial effects on combined mortality or severe brain injury among them (Table 40.2).17 Furthermore, only a limited number of infants born before 24 weeks of gestation were included in the study; therefore the basic approach toward selecting a delivery mode should be based on spontaneous delivery. Many perinatal centers, however, prefer to select cesarean section for several reasons, including less traumatic delivery for periviable infants, scheduling time,

Moreover, fluid intake during the first week of life should be less than the sum of insensible water loss and urine output, because fluid overload has been associated with an increased risk of patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) and necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC).27 An increase in environmental humidity, as close to 100% as possible, is the best strategy for fluid management in periviable preterm infants. Even under these circumstances, periviable preterm infants may show a body weight loss of over 20% during the first week of life.26

The concept of a respiratory strategy is based on maximum oxygenation with less likelihood of damage to vulnerable lungs; however, without positive pressure ventilation, severe apneic attacks could deteriorate not only respiratory conditions but also the infant’s general condition and lead to a vicious cycle. Although no standardized practice guidelines have been established, a noninvasive respiratory strategy cannot be applied to periviable preterm infants. Using available respiratory strategies, each hospital has its own strategy for respiratory care.35 A short intubation period was reported to be associated with the reduced incidence of bronchopulmonary dysplasia in very preterm infants.36 Despite this evidence, most periviable preterm infants were reported to be managed under mechanical ventilation.37 From a clinical point of view, therefore, periviable preterm infants should be managed with mechanical ventilation, which seems a much safer and more feasible method to ensure their survival.

### Circulatory Care: Role of Functional Echocardiography

Although all practices are essential for better outcomes in periviable preterm infants, circulatory care is especially crucial to support their survival. Respiratory care is mainly dependent on the lung maturation of preterm infants. If their lungs are not ready for gas exchange due to anatomical immaturity, no treatment can be effective to improve the respiratory condition. Even surfactant installation might not be successful if the lungs are not sufficiently matured to allow breathing at alveolar levels. On the other hand, there are many organized approaches to support the circulatory condition in periviable preterm infants. Furthermore, the Japanese approach to circulatory care in periviable preterm infants is unique in the following two features: hemodynamic management by functional echocardiography by neonatologists and timely use of hydrocortisone based on echocardiographic assessment.38 A main focus of this chapter is related to circulatory management in periviable preterm infants in Japan.

The concept of point-of-care functional echocardiography by neonatologists is familiar and has been well accepted in such areas as Australia, New Zealand, Canada, the United States, the United Kingdom, European countries, and Japan since the 2000s.39–45 The noninvasive nature of echocardiography is advantageous because it allows neonatologists to assess cardiovascular function

A calcium-induced calcium release (CICR) system in the mature myocardium can enhance the Ca++ release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum and make a rapid increase in concentration possible.49 In contrast, the CICR system in periviable preterm infants is not well developed, and the supply of Ca++ to the myofibrils relies mainly on direct extracellular calcium influx, resulting in a limited increase in intracellular Ca2++.49 In addition, the myocardium structure is also not well developed, which reduces its contractile ability as well as its diastolic expanding capacity compared to those in older preterm infants. Diffusion magnetic resonance imaging of the fetal heart shows the development of cardiomyocyte maturation and alignment.50 Maturation of the sheet structure of the heart occurs late in gestation, and it evolves further after birth. These findings indicate that periviable preterm infants are less adaptable to preload and afterload, which leads to a relatively flat response to the Frank-Starling law (Fig. 40.10).

Heart rate is regulated by the sympathetic and parasympathetic nerve systems. Heart rate variability analysis in preterm infants shows a low parasympathetic tone, which results in maintaining higher heart rates.51 Therefore periviable preterm infants have a limited ability to increase cardiac output by further increasing the heart rate.

Afterload is the backpressure to the left ventricle during myocardial contraction. The peripheral resistance of systemic circulation is a principal component in determining the degree of afterload. If the peripheral resistance overrides the myocardial contractility, left ventricular ejection capacity is impaired; however, blood pressure does not decrease but rather increases, followed by reduced peripheral perfusion. This means that blood pressure is not an adequate parameter for organ blood flow.51 In clinical practice, the left ventricular end-systolic wall stress (ESWS) calculated by echocardiography is used to evaluate afterload combined with rate-corrected mean velocity of fiber shortening (mVcfc).45 The ESWSmVcfc relation, also known as the stress-velocity relationship, shows an inverse correlation with a tendency of steepness in slope in preterm infants (Fig. 40.15).45,55,56 Therefore a so-called afterload mismatch in which cardiac output deteriorates severely in the face of increased afterload compared with cardiac contractility is not uncommon in periviable preterm infants.

As already mentioned, blood pressure is not a suitable indicator of organ blood flow. When afterload increases, a vasodilator should be used to decrease the peripheral resistance, which may decrease blood pressure and increase cardiac output consequently. • Fig. 40.7 Recommended algorithm of resuscitation for periviable preterm infants. There are no standardized procedures for the resuscitation of periviable preterm infants, but respiratory and circulatory stabilization immediately after birth should be prioritized. Without intensive support with positive pressure ventilation, the establishment of effective respiration among periviable preterm infants is not possible. The first step in postnatal stabilization is initiated by positive airway pressure with a bag and mask. When the heart rate increases above 100 beats per minute, tracheal intubation could be attempted. The initial fraction of inspired oxygen (IFiO2) should be between 0.3 and 0.6. Temperature control in infants is also critical for successful resuscitation. After the intratracheal tube is secured, the infant should be transferred to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) for further procedures. (Data from Kusuda S, Nakanishi H, Oei JL. • Fig. 40.10 Frank-Starling law: A heart with normal myocardial function can increase its output in response to an increased preload when all other factors remain constant (blue line). In contrast, an immature myocardial muscle can respond to a lesser degree due to the underdevelopment of muscle fibers (red line). Furthermore, if preload is exceeded or afterload is high, it may cause a limited response to preload (right portion of red line), resulting in congestive heart failure. The range within which periviable preterm infants can adapt their cardiac output depending on preload is limited, and the adjustment of preload using functional echocardiography is critical.

• Fig. 40.18 Enzymatic pathway of the fetal adrenal gland. Cortisol is essential for maintaining the vital activity of the fetus; conversely, excessive exposure is harmful to many organs, especially the developing brain. Therefore two mechanisms operate to protect the fetus from excessive cortisol exposure. The first is based on the suppression of 3β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase (3β-HSD), which is a key enzyme for cortisol production in the adrenal cortex. The progesterone necessary to produce cortisol is supplied through the placenta, thus the ability to produce cortisol is limited. The other mechanism is the conversion of cortisol from the mother through the placenta to an inactive form of cortisol, cortisone, by 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase (11β-HSD) type 2. After preterm birth, cortisol must be produced by preterm infants, but the activity of 3β-HSD remains suppressed, and the supply of progesterone from the placenta is stopped. When stress exceeds a certain level, cortisol production becomes insufficient in preterm infants, resulting in relative adrenal insufficiency. LDL, Low-density lipoprotein; DHA, dehydroepiandrosterone; DHAS, dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate. (Adapted from Masumoto K, Kusuda S. Hemodynamic support of the micropreemies; should hydrocortisone never be left out? Semin Fetal Neonatal Med. 2021;26:10,101222.)
