6 Importance of Simulation in Neonatology

### MONIKA BHOLA

### Introduction

Simulation has become a cornerstone of medical education and has its roots in the fundamental principles of “first do no harm” and “never the first time on a patient.” With the introduction of work hour rules, exposure to medical procedures and acute clinical situations has decreased significantly for trainees. Medical simulation provides a safe environment to practice repeatedly on mannequins and to master a technique before performing it for the first time on a patient. In the United States, there are approximately 400,000 preventable deaths and 3.5 million iatrogenic injuries causing disabilities every year.1 It has become ethically imperative to incorporate simulation into medical education.2 Simulation has become a reliable and proven tool to decrease errors in high reliability organizations and is now being enthusiastically embraced by the medical community. Simulation has become not just an effective tool but also an essential tool to mitigate errors and enhance safety. Learning is best facilitated when the learning opportunities are tailored to meet the needs of the learners. Training models that offer the same content in the same fashion to all learners (thus implying that competency can be attained and maintained simply by spending a particular, often arbitrary, amount of time at a task) fail to recognize that adults have different strengths, weaknesses, and life experiences and acquire and maintain different skills at different rates. Some characteristics of effective adult learning strategies include the following: • Focus on active rather than passive learning activities. • Integrate skill sets while performing under realistic

conditions. • Emphasize competency/proficiency (the ability to per-

form successfully) rather than compliance (adherence to rules, such as participation in an activity for a predetermined period of time). Simulation may be defined broadly as any exercise that allows an individual to experience a situation that, although not real, nevertheless generates authentic responses on his or her part.3 The best simulations realistically recreate the key

100

visual, auditory, and tactile cues of actual situations to provide experiences that closely mimic the conditions encountered when working in the real environment. Provision of these key cues creates a high level of fidelity to the real environment. Because of this degree of realism, those participating in the simulation respond as they would to a real-life situation; thus their performance during a simulated situation can be hypothesized to resemble what it would be in the real world. In general, the term simulation is typically reserved to describe comprehensive learning, assessment, and research/quality improvement activities that occur in realistic physical and virtual environments. Activities conducted in less pragmatic environments (case studies, roleplaying, and practice of one particular skill in isolation from other elements associated with that skill) may be better labeled as task training. By suspending their knowledge that they are working in a contrived situation, participants display knowledge, skills, and behaviors similar or identical to those manifest when working in the real environment. The closer the simulation mimics real life, the more likely the participant will be able to identify and address any weaknesses that become apparent. Identification and remediation of weaknesses is the raison d’être of simulation.

### History of Simulation

### Nonmedical Simulation

Even though medical simulation has existed in a very elemental form for centuries, other high reliability organizations have incorporated simulation in some form or another to increase operational safety in the past century. Edwin Link was the inventor of the first flight simulator in 1929; the “blue box” flight trainer was developed as a less expensive and safer method to learn to fly a plane.4 The army used these trainers after several aviation accidents in 1934. World War II saw an exponential increase in the use of these “blue boxes” and inspired other Link innovations. This simulation technology was adopted by civil aviation in 1955 and was required as an essential tool for recertification of commercial pilots’ licenses


## PDF page 120 | printed page 101

by the Federal Aviation Administration. The 1950s were characterized by the birth of analog computers and alliance with the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) in the 1960s, after the development of digital computers. This eventually led to the development of Apollo simulators that were the first to be fully digital. In the 1970s, the simulation technology was adopted by the field of power plants. The high number of reported deaths during flight training in the 1980s was the major impetus for extension of simulators to military aircrafts and naval submarines. By the 1990s, simulation was a standard form of training not just for the cockpit personnel but also for all the crew in the commercial airline industry. Despite the high cost of simulators, they have proven very cost-effective in terms of lives saved and flight equipment spared.4,5

The 1990s heralded the advent of high-resolution graphics in the gaming industry and creation of high-quality simulators, which became standard of training in high reliability organizations.

### Medical Simulation

Medical simulation was first described as early as 800 BCE, when an Indian surgeon, Sushruta, had his students practice incisions, ligation, and suturing on pieces of leather, tissue, or lotus leaves.

Prior to the advent of high-quality mannequins, actors were used to facilitate education in 1963. These actors were trained to portray different medical conditions. This eventually progressed to using “patient instructors,” where a patient with chronic stable findings was used to teach physical examination and diagnostic skills. This terminology was changed to standardized patients (SPs) and became an essential part of clinical examination in the 1990s.4

The present-day mannequins have their origins in the 1960s, when plastic human torso models were used for mouth-to-mouth resuscitation and chest compressions. Over the years, these have now evolved into varying degrees of high-fidelity simulators from initially being task trainers.

### Simulation-Based Training in Health Care

Much has been made of the importance of the concept of fidelity in simulation-based training in health care. Simulation fidelity is typically thought of in terms of its physical, biologic, and psychological elements. Physical fidelity refers to the realism of the physical space in which training occurs; this space is made to look real by including appropriate working medical equipment, fluids, pharmacologic agents, beds, and the other elements necessary for patient care. Biologic fidelity includes the patient simulators and SPs as well as human beings acting as confederates during the simulation, playing roles designed to assist the evolution of the scenario. Patient simulators have been described as high, medium, and low fidelity, but there is no standardized definition of simulator fidelity. In reality, no physical patient simulator currently in use bears close resemblance to

a human being, either in terms of anatomy or physiology. The use of the term high fidelity when describing the current generation of patient simulators more likely refers to high complexity or high cost rather than any intrinsic similarity to a living human being. Finally, all of the previously mentioned elements interact with the mindset brought into the scenario by the trainees to create a sense of realism or “psychological” fidelity. The overall goal of simulation-based training is to provide experiences that closely mimic the conditions encountered when working in the actual clinical environment. The major difference between the simulated environment and the real environment is the absence of live human patients. Although debate continues in the health care simulation community as to how much fidelity is necessary, some may argue that the higher the fidelity of the scenario to real life, the better the learning opportunity. It should be understood that as long as sufficient attention is paid to providing the key (not all) visual, auditory, and tactile cues for trainees, allowing them to form a shared mental model of the nature of the situation that they are facing, they will have the opportunity to work effectively to resolve the clinical problems that become manifest during the scenario and therefore achieve the learning objectives.6 This has been endorsed by the use of “low fidelity” mannequins in low-resource countries with improved early neonatal outcomes after delivery.7

### Neonatal Simulation

A simulation center has become an essential part of almost all medical and nursing schools; however, these centers are not easily accessible to trainees at the hospital. Nonacademic and smaller community hospitals have almost no access to large simulation centers yet are committed to providing education and high-quality medical care. It has become an expectation for all hospitals to provide some level of training to all medical personnel to enhance safety and teamwork. It is highly recommended that every team responding to deliveries and providing emergency care to newborns has some degree of ongoing simulation training. This could even be in the form of low-fidelity simulation.8,9

Every residency and neonatology fellowship program should make every attempt to incorporate simulation in all aspects of training.10

### Simulation in the Delivery Room

Approximately 4 million babies are born in the United States every year; only approximately 10% require some


## PDF page 121

degree of resuscitation, with 1% needing extensive resuscitative efforts such as chest compressions, intubation, or delivery of medication. The Neonatal Resuscitation Program (NRP) was established by the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) in 1987 to standardize the care of the newborns in the delivery room. The initial paradigm of a didactic program evolved into one with emphasis on technical skills using suitable task trainers. Dr. Louis Halamek et al. can be credited for establishing and disseminating the realistic technique of learning via simulation in the field of neonatology.3 In 2010, the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation (ILCOR) noted that a number of studies had demonstrated that the use of simulation-based learning methodologies enhances performance during simulated resuscitations.

ILCOR recommends that simulation, briefing, and debriefing techniques should be used during training when caring for simulated patients and in the course of clinical activities involving real patients to facilitate the acquisition and maintenance of the skills necessary for effective neonatal resuscitation. This recommendation continues to remain in effect with the publication of the 2020 ILCOR guidelines.11

In the past 20 years, the NRP has undergone a major shift in learning methodology. Instructors are now required to shift their role from that of a teacher responsible for imparting knowledge to trainees, to that of a facilitator who fosters acquisition of skills by trainees as they accept primary responsibility for their own learning. The NRP Steering Committee also developed a list of the characteristics desired in a cost-effective human neonatal patient simulator and published this online in 2005 as a request for proposals to industry. This marked the first time in the history of health care simulation that a professional body, rather than industry, drove development of a realistic patient simulator based on established learning objectives. Since that time, a number of full-term and preterm neonatal patient simulators have been developed by several commercial companies, indicative of the growth of the simulator industry at large and the field of neonatal simulation in particular.

In 2004, The Joint Commission (JC) published in a Sentinel Event Alert that ineffective communication was a major cause in almost 75% of the 47 cases of neonatal mortality or severe neonatal morbidity (lifelong serious neurologic compromise) reported to that agency; since then, an additional 62 cases have been added.12 In response to these root cause analyses, The JC recommended that all health care organizations responsible for delivering newborns “conduct team training in perinatal areas to teach staff to work together and communicate more effectively” and “for high-risk events, such as shoulder dystocia, emergency cesarean delivery, maternal hemorrhage, and neonatal resuscitation, conduct clinical drills to help staff prepare for when such events actually occur, and conduct debriefings to evaluate team performance and identify areas for improvement.” This recommendation laid the foundation for emphasis on key behavioral skills during neonatal resuscitation (Box 6.1).

Inclusion of the obstetric team is essential for building teamwork. Although very limited data of combined obstetrics and neonatal (OB-NEO) simulation are available regarding neonatal outcomes, there are some emerging data from Southeast Asia which demonstrate a decrease in morbidity with this OB-NEO collaboration.13

There is unequivocal evidence in the obstetric literature that simulation lab training translates to improved outcome at the bedside.14 Some of the most convincing work published to date regarding the value of simulationbased training involves the reduction in the incidence of serious sequelae of shoulder dystocia after implementation of comprehensive simulation-based training. In the Obstetrics Emergency Training Program at Southmead Hospital, Bristol, United Kingdom, Draycott and colleagues used a variety of patient simulators in several simulated environments to show that simulation-based learning resulted in enhanced content knowledge and improved technical management of shoulder dystocia.15–18 In a retrospective multicenter cohort observational study of 19,460 infants, (1) the incidence of infants born with 5-minute Apgar scores of 6 or less decreased from 86.6 to 44.6 per 10,000 births (P < .001), and (2) encephalopathy decreased from 27.3 to 13.6 per 10,000 births (P < .032) over a 5-year period following the introduction of a training program consisting of a review of fetal heart rate tracings and hands-on drills in the management of shoulder dystocia, postpartum hemorrhage, eclampsia, twin delivery, breech presentation, and maternal and neonatal resuscitation. This work has been replicated and shown to produce similar improvements in clinically relevant outcomes, serving to document the value of rigorous immersive training.19

There is a growing body of evidence for improved teamwork, confidence of providers, and improved procedural skills in the delivery room during resuscitation; however, none of these has shown long-term retention of skills, which demonstrates the need for high-frequency simulations even if they are of short duration and even for experienced providers.20,21

### Simulation in Neonatal Intensive Care Unit

Simulation has now moved from the delivery room to all aspects of neonatal care and medical care in general.22


## PDF page 122 | printed page 103

It is common practice to use simulation in varying degrees for all procedures, be they intubation, use of laryngeal mask airway, umbilical line placement, other central line placement, intraosseous needle placement, chest tube placement, paracentesis, or pericardiocentesis, among others. Opportunities to do actual procedures are extremely limited because of low frequency of most of these procedures and the added effect of work hour rules for the physician trainees.

“Just in time” procedural practice (reviewing and practicing the procedure on a mannequin) is gaining popularity because it helps refresh the technique just prior to the procedure and helps boost the confidence of the learner. Readily available task trainers in the work areas are being used to review certain procedures (intubation, spinal tap, umbilical lines) during a planned procedure.23–25

With the advent of single-room NICUs, new nurses have lost some of the continued mentorship of experienced nurses for bedside care. It has now become a growing practice for new nurses to perform intensive training through simulation, and there is a growing body of evidence in the nursing literature of its utility and effectiveness.26–29 Simulation continues to be used for improvement of skills and teamwork (e.g., extracorporeal membrane oxygenation [ECMO],34,36 reduction of central line infections, golden hour, and “small baby protocols”). It has also become an important part of education and is used in all aspects of patient safety and quality improvement endeavors as well as in neonatal transport.30–32,35 Simulation has also shown to be an effective tool to identify latent safety threats as demonstrated by Wong et al. during magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in neonatal patients.33

Simulation techniques have also permeated the world of communication and are being used for antenatal consults and even for training fellows and residents for “difficult conversations” with patients and families. There is a growing trend toward also incorporating this technology for education of home caregivers for technology-dependent patients.22,37,38

### Simulation in Low-Resourced Areas

Helping Babies Breathe (HBB) is an educational program that was developed by the AAP in 2010 designed to decrease neonatal mortality in low-resourced countries and has proven to be a powerful model for simulation success, despite the fact that it is low fidelity. Since its implementation in more than 80 countries, meta-analysis has demonstrated an average consistent reduction in fresh stillbirths of 34% (odds ratio [OR] 0.66; 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.52–0.85) and very early neonatal mortality an average of 30% (OR 0.70; 95% CI 0.51–0.98).39,40

Innovative virtual trainings are now being proven effective in retaining knowledge when in-person training is not feasible.

### Debriefing

A debriefing is defined as a discussion of events that have already occurred and can be led by an individual or a group.

This is in contrast to a briefing, which is held to discuss planned or future events. In debriefing of a health care team, the flow of information happens between the leader(s) and the members of the team, as well as among the members of the team. In contrast, feedback is the provision of information (from a person or a device) to a person in which the flow of that information is one way. Although not as interactive as a debriefing, it is important to note that not all learning requires the type of interaction typically ascribed to debriefing. For example, the acquisition and maintenance of a technical skill such as intubation do not require debriefing; rather, the provision of feedback as to appropriate finger and hand position on the laryngoscope and other aspects of intubation technique can be provided by an instructor experienced in intubation without the extensive interaction with trainees ascribed to debriefing.

Much of the health care literature has been devoted to the theoretical underpinnings of debriefing, rather than actual practical strategies and techniques, and it has focused on relatively inexperienced trainees rather than experienced health care professionals. Most of the models described in the literature use a very similar approach consisting of three primary phases: 1. An individual trained in debriefing who is not a member

of the team leads the discussion; 2. the discussion typically includes reaction, description,

analysis, and summary phases; and 3. patient outcome is not routinely emphasized (and may

actually be avoided if felt to potentially produce negative reactions in team members). This stands in contrast to how debriefing is conducted in other industries in which the risk to human life is high. In these industries, the emphasis of debriefing is on facts, not feelings, and objective outcomes, not subjective interpretations.3,41–45

There are several techniques for debriefing, including Promoting Excellence and Reflective Learning in Simulation (PEARLS), the three-dimensional (3D) model, and the diamond method, to name just a few.46,47 It is not uncommon for experienced debriefers to use a blended model of the various techniques. The debriefing model adopted by the NRP originated at the Center for Advanced Pediatric and Perinatal Education (CAPE), located at Packard Children’s Hospital in California. The CAPE debriefing model focuses on the actions of the individual, how those actions contribute to the performance of the team, and ultimately how team performance influences the care of the patient, therefore aligning closely with debriefing methods used by aerospace, commercial aviation, and the military. In this model, the person(s) leading the debriefing draws the assessment of individual and team performance from the team members themselves as they evaluate patient outcome in response to their actions and objectively analyze those actions. Upon the conclusion of the debriefing, team members must understand how actions beneficial to the patient can be replicated and how any actions that were nonproductive or harmful can be avoided. It is very important to understand the difference between a technical performance debriefing (used to assess human and system performance) and a critical incident stress debriefing (conducted to provide emotional/psychological support). A critical incident is defined as “an event that has the potential for people to experience significant physical, cognitive, emotional, and behavioral reactions immediately after the incident or days, weeks, months, and even sometimes years later.”48 If simulated clinical scenarios are welldesigned and the associated debriefings are conducted in an appropriate manner, a critical incident stress debriefing should very rarely be required; if it is, appropriate professional counseling should be arranged.

Although several studies have not consistently shown clear benefits to the use of video during debriefing, it must be acknowledged that, for the cognitive, technical, and behavioral skills of the team members to be effectively debriefed, an accurate record of the display of those skills must be available.49–54 Reliance on memory alone to provide such an accurate record is dubious at best. The ability to display a video stream illustrating key viewing angles coupled with an audio stream that picks up communication among team members provides an objective record of events that is capable of effectively bridging any gap between team members’ memories and the actual facts of the scenario. Incorporating debriefing into significant clinical events/ resuscitations will help learners accept this methodology as a “routine” learning and reflective tool without the fear of any negative judgment or repercussions.55,56
