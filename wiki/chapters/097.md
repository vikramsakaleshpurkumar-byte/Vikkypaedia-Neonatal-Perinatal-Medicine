### BETH A. VOGT

In the past 30 years, the field of clinical neonatal nephrology has expanded significantly in concert with major advances and changes in the care and survival of neonates, particularly premature neonates. The widespread use of prenatal ultrasonography has expanded early identification of congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract (CAKUT), many of which are caused by mutations in key developmental genes. Because of the wide spectrum of CAKUT, the new challenge for providers is to determine which infants with a history of abnormal antenatal imaging need evaluation and intervention and which have benign conditions that will be of little clinical significance. Acute kidney injury (AKI) has been increasingly recognized as a common and important condition in the hospitalized neonate which is associated with increased length of hospitalization, mortality, and risk for later development of chronic kidney disease (CKD). Neonatal hypertension remains an important complication of umbilical artery catheters, but there is now increased recognition of hypertension associated with bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) support, or idiopathic causes.

The goals of this chapter are to review the anatomic and functional development of the kidney, outline the recommended approach to the evaluation of the neonate with suspected renal disease, and provide an overview of the common nephrologic and urologic problems seen in preterm and term neonates.

### Kidney and Urinary Tract Development

The development of the kidney and urinary tract is a carefully coordinated process that involves the regulated activation and inactivation of hundreds of genes that encode transcription factors, growth factors and receptors, structural proteins, adhesion molecules, and other regulatory proteins.1 Genetic, environmental, and epigenetic factors that interrupt this complex process can lead to a spectrum of congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract (CAKUT), described later in this chapter.

### Development of the Kidney

Human kidney development begins in the first trimester of pregnancy. There are three stages of development: the pronephros, mesonephros, and metanephros (illustrated in Fig. 97.1). The pronephros develops in the cervical region and disappears by the end of the fourth week of gestation. As the pronephros regresses, the mesonephros appears caudally to it, and, by the end of the eighth week of gestation, most portions of the mesonephros disappear.

The metanephros, which ultimately becomes the definitive kidney, begins forming at 5 weeks of gestation. A subset of cells from the metanephric mesenchyme secretes glial-like derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF), a signal that initiates budding of the ureteric bud (UB) from the mesonephric duct. The UB then invades the metanephric mesenchyme and induces cells to undergo mesenchymal-epithelial transformation to begin formation of fetal kidney. Branching morphogenesis occurs, dependent on back and forth signaling between the UB and the metanephric mesenchyme. The metanephros develops through a series of transitional stages and ultimately develops into the glomeruli and tubules which comprise the upper part of the nephron. The UB forms the lower part of the nephron (collecting ducts), as well as the renal pelvis and ureters.

The metanephric kidney becomes functional during the second half of pregnancy. Fetal urine becomes a major contributor to amniotic fluid at 16 to 20 weeks of gestation. Nephrogenesis, the formation of new nephron units, is complete at 34 weeks of gestation, when each kidney contains its definitive complement of approximately 800,000 to 1.2 million nephrons.1 Infants of mothers with nutritional deficiency, placental insufficiency, or those born before completion of nephrogenesis can be born with a suboptimal number of nephrons and be at risk for future development of CKD or hypertension.2,3

A number of prenatal factors can impair normal development of the urinary tract and have been associated with increased risk of CAKUT. Maternal factors include maternal diabetes mellitus, older maternal age, gestational hypertension, and obesity.7,8 Angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors (ACE-I) and angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARB) can lead to renin angiotensin system blocker fetopathy, which presents with renal tubular dysgenesis, neonatal anuria, pulmonary hypoplasia, and occasional central nervous system abnormalities, most often after exposure during the second trimester of pregnancy.9 Other potential exposures with an adverse effect on urinary tract development include excessive alcohol consumption, maternal vitamin A or folate deficiency, maternal use of cocaine, use of immunosuppressant medications, use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and heavy metal exposure.7,10

### Renal Blood Flow

Absolute renal blood flow (RBF) and the percentage of cardiac output directed to the kidneys increase steadily with advancing gestational age. The kidneys of a human fetus receive approximately 3% to 5% of the cardiac output. The relatively low RBF of the fetus is related to high renovascular resistance caused by the increased activity of the reninangiotensin-aldosterone and sympathetic nervous systems. The RBF increases dramatically postnatally, reaching 10% of the cardiac output at 1 week of life, and achieves adult values of 25% of the cardiac output by 2 years of age. This rise in RBF is caused primarily by decreasing renovascular resistance and increasing cardiac output and perfusion pressure.

### Concentration and Dilution of Urine

Newborn infants have a limited capacity to concentrate their urine, with maximal urinary osmolality of 800 mOsm/ kg in a term infant compared to that of a 2-year-old, which approximates adult values of 1400 mOsm/kg. In contrast, the term newborn infant has full ability to maximally dilute its urine in response to a water load, achieving adult values of 50 mOsm/kg. Preterm infants are unable to fully dilute their urine but can achieve urine osmolality of 70 mOsm/ kg. However, the response of premature infants to an acute water load may be limited because of their low GFR and the decreased activity of sodium transporters in the diluting segment of the nephron. The excessive administration of water may place the newborn infant at a high risk for dilutional hyponatremia and hypervolemia. Urinary diluting and concentrating capacity in term and preterm infants are discussed in more detail in Chapter 96.

### Laboratory Evaluation Urinalysis

The examination of a freshly voided urine specimen provides immediate, valuable information about the condition of the kidneys. A urine specimen collected by cleaning the perineum and applying a sterile adhesive plastic bag is useful for obtaining a sample for urinalysis but is not recommended for urine culture, because it may be associated with a false-positive result caused by fecal contamination. Bladder catheterization is a more reliable method for collecting a sample for culture but may be technically difficult in preterm infants. Suprapubic bladder aspiration is an alternative urinary collection method in preterm infants without intraabdominal pathology or bleeding disorders.

Analysis of the urine should include inspection, urinary dipstick assessment, and microscopic analysis. The newborn urine is usually clear and nearly colorless. Cloudiness may represent a urinary tract infection or the presence of crystals. A yellow-brown to deep olive-green color may represent large amounts of conjugated bilirubin. Porphyrins, certain drugs such as phenytoin, bacteria, and urate crystals may stain the diaper pink and be confused with bleeding. Brown urine may suggest AKI, hemoglobinuria, or myoglobinuria.

The specific gravity of neonatal urine is usually very low (<1.004) but may be factitiously elevated by high molecular weight solutes such as contrast agents, glucose or other reducing substances, or large amounts of protein. Urinary osmolality may be a more reliable measurement of the concentrating and diluting capacity of the kidney. Urinary dipstick evaluation can detect the presence of heme-containing compounds (i.e., red blood cells, myoglobin, and hemoglobin), protein, and glucose. White blood cell products such as leukocyte esterase and nitrite may also be detected on urinary dipstick evaluation and should raise the suspicion of urinary tract infection, mandating collection of a urine culture. The microscopic examination of urinary sediment may detect the presence of red blood cells, white blood cells, bacteria, casts, or crystals.

### Assessment of Renal Function

Although 98% of term infants void during the first 30 hours of life,15 a delay in urination for up to 48 hours should not be a cause for immediate concern in the absence of a palpable bladder, abdominal mass, or other signs or symptoms of renal disease. A failure to void for longer than 48 hours may suggest impairment of renal function and should prompt further investigation. The serum creatinine level is the simplest and most commonly used test used to estimate neonatal kidney function.16,17 Immediately after birth, the serum creatinine concentration reflects the maternal creatinine concentration. In term infants, the serum creatinine level gradually decreases from a range of 0.6 to 1 mg/dL (depending on the mother’s serum creatinine) at birth to a mean value of 0.4 mg/dL within the first 2 weeks of life (see Table 97.2). In preterm infants, the decline in serum creatinine level is slower and may not

reach nadir for 1 to 2 months. In very preterm infants, the creatinine may actually rise transiently because of low GFR and tubular reabsorption of creatinine before falling to reach a nadir value at 2 months of age (Table 97.3).18 Failure of the serum creatinine level to fall or a persistent increase in serum creatinine suggests impairment of renal function. Estimation of GFR is difficult in neonates, because formulas used in children have not been validated in infants less than 1 year of age. Measurement of serum cystatin C, a low molecular weight protein produced in all cells and filtered through the glomerulus, may offer more accurate estimation of neonatal kidney function but is not yet widely available for use.16,19

### Radiologic Evaluation

Ultrasonography is the most common method of imaging the neonatal urinary tract because it is a readily available, noninvasive test that does not involve exposure to contrast agents or radiation. Ultrasonography is indicated in infants with a history of an abnormal antenatal ultrasound, as well as in infants with an abdominal mass, AKI, hypertension, hematuria, or congenital malformations or genetic syndromes with increased risk of urinary tract anomalies. Ultrasonography can identify hydronephrosis, cystic kidney disease, and abnormalities of kidney size and position. It also may be used as a screening tool in preterm infants at increased risk of developing nephrocalcinosis because of chronic lung disease and long-term loop diuretic therapy. A Doppler flow study of the renal arteries and aorta may be helpful in the evaluation of infants with suspected renovascular hypertension, renal arterial or venous thrombosis, or AKI.

Importantly, nonurgent postnatal ultrasounds, such as those performed to follow up mild to moderate antenatal hydronephrosis, should be delayed until at least 48 hours after birth. Ultrasounds performed in the first 24 to 48 hours of life may be falsely reassuring because of the low urine output of the neonate in the first few days of life.

Voiding cystourethrography (VCUG) should be considered an important part of the radiologic examination in infants with significant hydronephrosis, hydroureter, or documented urinary tract infection. VCUG involves the instillation of a radiopaque contrast agent into the bladder by urinary catheterization. This study is the procedure of choice to evaluate the urethra and bladder and ascertain the presence or absence of VUR and posterior urethral valves (PUV). Other radiologic tests may occasionally be used for diagnostic purposes in the neonate (see Chapter 38). Radioisotopic renal scanning may be of value in locating anomalous kidneys, identifying obstruction or renal scarring, or determining the contribution of each kidney to overall renal function; note that these tests may be difficult to interpret in the first month of life because of the low GFR in newborns. Abdominal computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging is useful in the diagnosis of renal tumors or abscesses.

### Clinical Problems

### Hematuria

Hematuria can be broadly classified into two categories: microscopic and macroscopic (gross). Microscopic hematuria is defined as ≥5 red blood cells on high-powered examination of the urine without visible discoloration of the urine. Macroscopic hematuria is defined as visibly discolored (red or brown) urine with ≥5 red blood cells on high-powered examination of the urine. It is important to confirm that discolored urine is caused by hematuria and not caused by conditions such as urate crystals, bile

pigments, and porphyrins; in these situations the urinary dipstick test is negative for blood and the microscopic examination reveals no red blood cells. In addition, there are two conditions that cause red or brown urine with a positive urine dipstick test for blood, but no red blood cells on microscopic examination. These include myoglobinuria caused by inherited metabolic myopathy, infectious myositis, or rhabdomyolysis and hemoglobinuria caused by ABO incompatibility or other hemolytic disease. Vaginal bleeding or skin breakdown from diaper dermatitis can also mimic gross hematuria.

Once it is determined that an infant has macroscopic hematuria, there is a broad differential diagnosis.20 Urinary tract infection should be ruled out with urine culture. Acute tubular necrosis (ATN) or cortical necrosis should be considered in infants with a history of perinatal asphyxia. Renal venous thrombosis must be considered in infants with risk factors, including birth to a diabetic mother, cyanotic congenital heart disease, volume depletion, or an indwelling umbilical venous catheter. Urolithiasis or nephrocalcinosis should be suspected in at-risk infants with a history of chronic lung disease and loop diuretic use. Other causes include trauma from bladder catheterization or suprapubic aspiration, obstructive uropathy, tumors, or hemorrhagic disease of the newborn in infants who have not received vitamin K prophylaxis.

Evaluation of the neonate with macroscopic hematuria includes formal urinalysis with microscopic examination, catheterized urine culture, complete blood count (CBC), serum electrolytes, creatinine, and kidney/bladder ultrasound. Further evaluation may include coagulation profile, urine calcium-to-creatinine ratio, abdominal computerized tomography scan, or urologic evaluation.

### Proteinuria

Proteinuria is defined as a urinary dipstick value of at least 1+ (30 mg/dL), with a specific gravity of 1.015 or less, or a urinary dipstick value of at least 2+ (100 mg/dL), with a specific gravity of more than 1.015. Normal urinary protein-to-creatinine ratio is less than 0.5 mg/mg in infants and toddlers younger than 2 years of age. Nearly any form of kidney injury can result in a transient increase in urinary protein excretion, including ATN, fever, dehydration, cardiac failure, high doses of penicillin, and the administration of a contrast agent. Persistent heavy proteinuria, edema, and hypoalbuminemia in a neonate should prompt the consideration of congenital nephrotic syndrome, an autosomal recessive disorder characterized by massive proteinuria, failure to thrive, a large placenta, and CKD (see section later in chapter). Proteinuria associated with glucosuria, phosphaturia, and metabolic acidosis raises concern for Fanconi syndrome, a rare condition characterized by generalized proximal tubular dysfunction; possible causes include cystinosis, Lowe syndrome, and mitochondrial disorders. False positive urinary dipstick values for protein may be the result of highly concentrated urine, alkaline urine, infection, and detergents.

### Glycosuria

The diagnosis of glycosuria is established by the presence of glucose on a urinary dipstick. Glycosuria frequently occurs in infants with hyperglycemia, as the serum glucose exceeds the renal threshold for reabsorption. Common causes of hyperglycemia and resultant glycosuria in the NICU include sepsis or the administration of total parenteral nutrition (TPN). Correction of hyperglycemia normalizes the urinary dipstick findings. Isolated glycosuria with a normal serum glucose concentration raises concern for renal glycosuria, a benign inherited condition caused by an abnormality in the proximal tubular transport of glucose. No therapy is necessary other than the recognition of the condition, avoidance of confusion with diabetes mellitus, and provision of a normal intake of carbohydrates.

If glycosuria is accompanied by other evidence of renal tubular dysfunction, such as an excessive urinary loss of potassium, phosphorus, and amino acids, a generalized proximal tubulopathy (e.g., Fanconi syndrome) should be considered. Glycosuria may also be seen in infants with congenital renal diseases such as renal dysplasia, in which there is significant tubular dysfunction. Glycosuria in an infant with severe, watery diarrhea should raise the suspicion of congenital intestinal glucose-galactose malabsorption syndrome.

### Intrinsic (Renal) Acute Kidney Injury

The most common cause of intrinsic AKI in neonates is ATN. Risk factors for ATN include a prolonged prerenal state, perinatal asphyxia, sepsis, neonatal cardiac surgery, need for ECMO support, and nephrotoxic drug administration (acyclovir, aminoglycoside antibiotics, amphotericin B, ACE inhibitors, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), radiocontrast agents, and vancomycin). The pathophysiology of ATN is complex and appears to involve interrelationships among renal tubular cellular injury, hypoxia, and altered glomerular filtration and hemodynamics. Other causes of intrinsic AKI in the newborn include renal dysplasia, autosomal recessive polycystic kidney disease (ARPKD), and renal arterial or venous thrombosis. Glomerulonephritis, although a common cause of AKI in older children and adolescents, is very uncommon in the neonatal population.

### Obstructive (Postrenal) Acute Kidney Injury

Obstructive AKI in the neonate may be related to a variety of congenital urinary tract conditions, including PUV, bilateral ureteropelvic or ureterovesical junction obstruction,

and neurogenic bladder. ### Medical Management

In the case of established oliguric AKI, a urinary catheter should be placed to exclude LUTO. If there is no improvement in urine output after bladder drainage is established, a fluid challenge of 10 to 20 mL/kg should be administered over 1 to 2 hours to exclude prerenal AKI. Consideration should be given to use of vasopressor support such as dopamine to ensure that the infant has a mean arterial pressure adequate to provide renal perfusion. A lack of improvement in urine output and serum creatinine following adequate bladder drainage, fluid resuscitation, and establishment of an adequate mean arterial pressure suggests intrinsic AKI.

The goal of medical management of intrinsic AKI is to provide supportive care until there is spontaneous improvement in kidney function. After adequate resuscitation, intake should be restricted to insensible losses (500 mL/m2/ day, or 30 mL/kg/day) plus urine output and other measured losses (see Chapter 96) to prevent symptomatic fluid overload. Daily to twice daily weights and careful intake and output measurements are essential to follow volume status. Nephrotoxic drugs should be discontinued to reduce the risk of additional kidney injury. Medications should be adjusted by dose, interval, or both according to the degree of kidney dysfunction. Potassium and phosphorus should be restricted in those neonates with hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, oliguria, and/or rapidly worsening kidney function. Metabolic acidosis may require treatment with intravenous or oral sodium bicarbonate. Loop diuretics may prove helpful in augmenting the urinary flow rate but should be withheld if there is no response or in the case of anuria.

The chief advantage of hemodialysis is the ability to rapidly remove solutes and fluid. The main disadvantages are the requirement for central vascular access, and the hemodynamic instability and osmolar shifts that may occur with rapid solute and fluid shifts.

Providing adequate RRT to an infant in the NICU may be limited by the challenges in placing and/or maintaining intravascular or PD access in the very small premature neonate. If dialysis access cannot be established, care of the infant with AKI is limited to maximal supportive medical management, with meticulous attention to fluid, electrolyte, and acid–base balance.

### Prognosis

Research has demonstrated that AKI is not just a marker of severity of illness in neonates but is also independently associated with poor outcomes. A large multicenter retrospective study demonstrated that the presence of AKI was independently associated with increased mortality in neonates (10% vs. 1%), as well as increased length of hospital stay (median 23 vs. 19 days).23 In addition, infants with higher stages of AKI had higher mortality rates and lengths of hospitalization when compared to infants with lower stages of AKI.23

In the past, AKI was thought of as a completely reversible condition, with the idea that the return of serum creatinine to baseline indicated no further renal risk. However, observational studies have demonstrated high rates of CKD in survivors of neonatal AKI.33 The risk of CKD in survivors of neonatal AKI may be compounded by the independent risks of prematurity and low birth weight, both of which are associated with CKD because of associated inadequate nephron development.2,3 Outpatient nephrology follow-up care is

important for all neonates with an identified episode of significant AKI to monitor for proteinuria, hypertension, and rise in serum creatinine over time indicating development of CKD. Special attention should be given to those infants with a history of AKI stages 2 and 3, treatment with dialysis, significant prematurity, intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), or underlying urinary tract anomalies. Early identification of CKD allows implementation of strategies to slow the loss of GFR, including healthy lifestyle, careful management of BP, reduction of proteinuria, hydration, and avoidance of nephrotoxin exposure.

### Hypertension Incidence and Definition

The incidence of hypertension in neonates is estimated to be approximately 1% to 2%.34 Hypertension is most often seen in infants with a concurrent condition such as chronic lung disease, kidney disease, or a history of umbilical arterial catheterization. The definition of neonatal hypertension is sustained blood pressure above the 95th percentile for infants of similar size, gestational age, and postnatal age. Identification of elevated blood pressure in neonates can be difficult because of variations in blood pressure measurement techniques, normal changes in blood pressure with gestational age and weight, and other factors that affect blood pressure readings, including level of wakefulness, feeding, crying, and pain.

Useful data on normal infant blood pressures were published by Zubrow and colleagues, who prospectively measured blood pressure in nearly 700 infants, showing that blood pressure increased with gestational age, postconceptual age, and birth weight.35 Based on these data and synthesis of several other studies, a practical table of systolic, diastolic, and mean arterial blood pressure values was created and may be useful in defining normal and elevated blood pressures in neonates greater than 2 weeks of age from 26 to 44 weeks’ postconceptual age (Table 97.6).34

The majority of neonates with hypertension are asymptomatic, as elevated blood pressures are typically noted on routine monitoring. ### Prognosis

Infants with neonatal hypertension should have regular outpatient nephrology follow-up and blood pressure monitoring. Most neonates with hypertension associated with umbilical artery catheters, BPD, or of unknown cause experience improvement in their blood pressures and do not typically require antihypertensive medications beyond 6 to 12 months of age. Infants may require periodic increases in their antihypertensive medications following discharge from the hospital because of rapid growth, but antihypertensive agents may often be weaned by maintaining a stable medication dose as the infant grows in size. On the other hand, infants with hypertension related to a secondary cause

such as kidney disease will most likely have hypertension that persists into childhood.

Long-term studies of children and adolescents with a history of neonatal hypertension are needed. There is growing evidence that neonates born before completion of nephrogenesis at 34 to 36 weeks’ gestation may be at increased risk for development of hypertension and CKD in adolescence or adulthood.2,3

### Nephrocalcinosis

Nephrocalcinosis, defined as calcium salt deposition in the renal interstitium, is a well-known complication in the neonatal population, occurring in 7% to 41% of hospitalized premature infants.39 Infants at highest risk appear to be those with lower gestational age and birth weight, often in combination with severe respiratory disease. Nephrocalcinosis is usually discovered incidentally on abdominal imaging studies, although infants with nephrocalcinosis may present with microscopic or gross hematuria, granular material in the diaper, or urinary tract infection.

Neonatal nephrocalcinosis is multifactorial in etiology and develops as the consequence of an imbalance between stone-promoting and stone-inhibiting factors.39 The immature renal tubules of the preterm infant are more susceptible to crystal formation and aggregation. Furthermore, the low GFR of the preterm infant results in a low tubular fluid flow rate that promotes crystal formation. Hypercalciuria (excessive urinary calcium excretion) is a common risk factor and may result from chronic treatment with loop diuretics, increased calcium intake from TPN, or vitamin D supplementation. Subcutaneous fat necrosis following birth trauma or therapeutic hypothermia is a rare but serious cause of hypercalciuria.40 Other risk factors for nephrocalcinosis include hypocitraturia (low urinary citrate excretion); this is a common finding in patients with persistent metabolic acidosis, which often occurs in ill preterm infants. Less common genetic conditions in which there is an increased risk of nephrocalcinosis include primary hyperoxaluria, distal renal tubular acidosis (RTA), Bartter syndrome, Williams syndrome, and idiopathic infantile hypercalcemia.

Routine ultrasonographic screening should be considered for all infants who receive long-term loop diuretics for chronic lung or heart disease. In those with documented nephrocalcinosis, loop diuretics should be discontinued, if possible. Thiazide diuretics, such as chlorothiazide, may be substituted because they tend to reduce urinary calcium excretion, although serum calcium must be monitored closely to avoid hypercalcemia. Agents that may increase urinary calcium excretion, including glucocorticoids, xanthine derivatives, and any sodium-containing supplements should be minimized or discontinued if possible. Administration of calcium and phosphorus in TPN as well as vitamin D supplementation should be reduced if excessive. A high urinary flow rate should be maintained to reduce the probability of urinary crystallization. Metabolic acidosis, if

Serial renal ultrasonography is needed to confirm resolution of nephrocalcinosis. ### Renal Vascular Thrombosis

Thromboembolic complications are rare but serious events in the NICU. The neonatal kidney is particularly vulnerable to thrombosis because of its relatively low blood flow, small vessel diameter, enhanced renal vasoconstriction, and intervening events including hypoxia, volume depletion, hypotension, and infection that disturb the delicate balance between procoagulant and anticoagulant factors. Both venous and arterial thrombosis may occur in the neonate, although renal venous thromboses (RVTs) are more common. Risk factors for RVT include prematurity, maternal diabetes mellitus, congenital heart disease, polycythemia, hypertonic dehydration, acute blood loss, perinatal asphyxia, in utero death of a twin, sepsis, respiratory distress syndrome, and prolonged central venous cannulation.43 A subset of neonates with RVT have an inherited thrombophilia, including deficiencies of protein C, protein S or antithrombin, factor V Leiden mutation, or the prothrombin gene mutation.

Thrombosis begins in the small renal veins and propagates toward the main renal vein, ultimately reaching the inferior vena cava. Thrombosis is usually unilateral (70%) but may be bilateral or extend into the inferior vena cava and is associated with adrenal infarction in a minority of patients.

The classic clinical triad of symptoms in RVT includes a flank mass, macroscopic hematuria, and thrombocytopenia, although all elements of the triad are present in fewer than 25% of infants with RVT. Additional signs may include oliguric AKI, hypertension, vomiting, lethargy, anorexia, fever, and shock. Ultrasonography in the acute phase reveals enlarged and echogenic kidneys with loss of corticomedullary differentiation. A Doppler study may reveal resistance or absence of blood flow in the main renal vein and collateral vessels. Uptake may be absent or severely diminished on a radionuclide scan. Ultrasonography following the acute phase may show gradual decrease in kidney size with renal atrophy.

Initial evaluation after identification of a thrombosis should include baseline testing, including a CBC, prothrombin time, activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT), and fibrinogen concentration. Maternal blood may be tested for lupus anticoagulant and anticardiolipin antibody. Testing for an inherited prothrombotic state should be conducted in neonates with clinically significant thrombosis but is best deferred until after resolution of the acute event.

There are no evidence-based guidelines for management of neonatal RVT, although expert opinion guidelines suggest the need for a multidisciplinary team including neonatology, nephrology, radiology, and hematology. If the thrombosis appears to be catheter-associated, the catheter should be removed. Supportive medical treatment consists of correction of fluid and electrolyte imbalances as well as supportive treatment of AKI, possibly including dialysis. Anticoagulation (unfractionated heparin or low molecular weight heparin) may be used to prevent the extension of thrombosis, although the benefits of anticoagulation should be weighed against the risks of hemorrhagic events, including intraventricular hemorrhage. Thrombolytic therapies such as recombinant tissue plasminogen activator or urokinase should be reserved for cases of critical compromise of kidney function, as seen in bilateral renal vein thrombosis. Surgical thrombectomy is considered in patients with thrombosis of the inferior vena cava, and nephrectomy may be necessary in infants with severe refractory hypertension.

Although the perinatal mortality rate in infants with RVT has decreased progressively during the past decades, the prognosis for the affected kidney is poor, with progressive atrophy in up to 70% of kidneys.43 Long-term complications include hypertension in 20% of patients, CKD, and end-stage kidney disease in the minority of patients with a history of bilateral thrombosis.

### Renal Agenesis

Unilateral renal agenesis (URA; congenital absence of the kidney) occurs in 1 of 2000 births and is one of the most common forms of CAKUT.44 URA occurs when the ureteric bud fails to induce proper differentiation of the metanephric mesenchyme, an event that may be related to both genetic and environmental factors. URA may occur in healthy infants and is more common in males and on the left side. Birth registry data suggest that about 30% of infants with URA have extra-renal anomalies, including cardiac in 60% and skeletal in 30%.44 URA may be seen in infants with a variety of syndromes including VACTERL association (i.e., vertebral abnormalities, anal atresia, cardiac abnormalities, tracheoesophageal fistula or esophageal atresia, renal agenesis and dysplasia, and limb defects), caudal

regression syndrome, and branchio-oto-renal (BOR) syndrome. Approximately 30% of girls with URA have an associated Müllerian anomaly, which is usually clinically silent until puberty when girls may present with amenorrhea or dysmenorrhea.45 In males, congenital absence of the vas deferens along with URA has been reported.46

In patients with URA, it is important to carefully assess the health of the remaining solitary kidney; the presence of small kidney size, renal cysts, an echogenic appearance, or hydronephrosis suggests significant kidney disease. If this is the case, early involvement of a nephrologist and/or urologist is recommended. If the solitary kidney appears healthy, serial ultrasounds should demonstrate appropriate compensatory hypertrophy, with the kidney growing beyond the 95th percentile for age over time. In some patients, compensatory hypertrophy may be evident as early as 20 weeks’ gestation.47 The presence of hypertrophy in a solitary kidney is an important clinical indicator in terms of prognosis for good long-term kidney function. Most clinicians now reserve VCUG for patients with URA who have moderate to severe hydronephrosis, bladder abnormality, or develop a febrile urinary tract infection. Long-term nephrology follow-up is recommended for children with URA to screen for complications of reduced nephron number, including renal dysfunction, proteinuria, and hypertension.48

Bilateral renal agenesis (BRA) occurs in 1 of 4000 to 10,000 births.44 This diagnosis is generally made on early prenatal ultrasonography with the absence of kidneys, bladder, and anhydramnios. Historically, this diagnosis was incompatible with life, although with recent fetal interventions such as serial amnioinfusions, intensive ventilatory support after birth, and neonatal dialysis, the prognosis has improved somewhat, although with significant lifelong health burden.

### Multicystic Dysplastic Kidney

MCDK is one of the most common forms of CAKUT, occurring in 1 in 1000 to 4300 live births.49 MCDK is a nonfunctional kidney composed of multiple large cysts and no normal renal parenchyma (Fig. 97.4). With the widespread use of prenatal ultrasonography, MCDK is usually detected prenatally. MCDK has a male predominance and occurs twice as often on the left side as the right side. The pathogenesis likely involves abnormal interaction between the ureteric bud and the mesonephric mesenchyme, leading to failure of normal kidney development.49 MCDK often occurs sporadically but can be seen in infants with a number of congenital syndromes. Mutations in a number of renal developmental genes have been identified in patients with MCDK (see Table 97.1). About 20% of children with unilateral MCDK will have VUR into the contralateral kidney, which is usually low grade and is often clinically insignificant.

### Renal Dysplasia

Renal dysplasia is characterized by abnormal renal development in the fetus, leading to replacement of the renal parenchyma by cartilage and disorganized epithelial structures. The pathogenesis of renal dysplasia may involve mutations in developmental genes (see Table 97.1), urinary tract obstruction, or in utero toxin/medication exposure.8 Renal dysplasia may occur as an isolated finding but also occurs in infants with obstructive uropathy or congenital disorders including VACTERL association; CHARGE syndrome; BOR syndrome; Jeune syndrome; and trisomies 13, 18, and 21. Most forms of “polycystic kidneys” that are reported prenatally or in association with syndromes, in fact, represent cystic dysplasia. The function of dysplastic kidneys is quite variable; some infants may have well preserved kidney function into adulthood and others may exhibit signs of renal insufficiency as early as the first few days of life.

### Congenital Hydronephrosis

Hydronephrosis, defined as dilation of the upper urinary tract (Fig. 97.5), is one of the most common congenital findings detected by prenatal ultrasonography and is seen in 1% to 2% of pregnancies.52 Hydronephrosis may be seen in a wide spectrum of conditions, ranging from transient physiologic hydronephrosis to high grade VUR or urinary tract obstruction (Box 97.7). The challenge for the provider is predicting which infants are at risk of significant urinary tract disease while avoiding unnecessary testing and caregiver anxiety in those likely to have a benign condition. In general, the likelihood of clinically important urinary tract disease is directly correlated to the severity of antenatal hydronephrosis. In addition, the presence of bilateral hydronephrosis, progression of dilation, ureteral dilatation, renal parenchymal disease, bladder abnormalities, or oligohydramnios raises concern for significant CAKUT.

### Vesicoureteral Reflux

VUR is a condition seen in 1% to 2% of children; it may be an isolated condition or associated with CAKUT including PUV, EBS, and renal dysplasia (see below). VUR is defined as the backward movement of urine from the bladder to the upper urinary tract during voiding. VUR is believed to be related to ectopic insertion of the ureter into the bladder wall, resulting in a shorter intravesicular ureter, which acts as an incompetent valve during urination. VUR is diagnosed by VCUG and is graded from I to V, with grade I indicating the mildest form and grade V the most severe form. The degree of hydronephrosis correlates poorly with the grade of VUR and postnatal ultrasound is considered a poor screening test for the presence or absence of VUR. Although the exact genetic basis of this condition has not been determined, there appears to be a genetic component, because the incidence of VUR increased in first-degree relatives, including siblings. Table 97.1 lists a number of mutations which have been associated with VUR. VUR usually resolves over time as the intravesical segment of the ureter elongates with growth.

The primary risk for infants with VUR is development of febrile urinary tract infections (UTIs). Although in the past, most infants with hydronephrosis underwent a VCUG, this testing is now reserved for infants with suspected bladder outlet obstruction, severe hydronephrosis or following the first febrile UTI. Antibiotic prophylaxis may be useful for those with higher grade (III, IV, V) VUR. Infants with VUR should be followed by a nephrologist or urologist and may need surgical correction if VUR does not resolve

spontaneously or if repeated febrile UTIs occur despite antibiotic prophylaxis. Long-term concerns include development of hypertension, renal scarring, and progressive CKD because of associated renal dysplasia or infection-related scarring (“reflux nephropathy”).

### Posterior Urethral Valves

PUV represent the most common cause of LUTO, with an incidence of 1 in 5000 to 8000 live male births. A posterior urethral valve is a congenital membrane that fails to involute during fetal development and obstructs or partially obstructs the posterior urethra, variably affecting normal development of the bladder and upper urinary tract. Findings on prenatal ultrasound may include moderate to severe hydronephrosis; dilated ureters; a thickened, trabeculated bladder; dilated proximal urethra; and oligohydramnios. The antenatal presentation may include a palpable, distended bladder, poor urinary stream, and signs and symptoms of renal and pulmonary insufficiency. The VCUG is diagnostic for PUV and reveals associated VUR in 30% of patients.

There are now several treatment options for fetuses identified to have severe LUTO. These options include vesicoamniotic shunting and fetal cystoscopy with valve ablation;53,54 both interventions have the purpose of preserving amniotic fluid volume to allow fetal lung development and optimizing kidney function and development. Criterion for prenatal intervention include normal karyotype, no other anatomic abnormality, ultrasound findings of bladder obstruction, oligohydramnios or anhydramnios, and “favorable” urine studies. The benefits of prenatal intervention for LUTO are promising, but because of the inherent risks involved, such interventions must be limited to centers with extensive expertise in prenatal intervention.

Postnatal treatment is centered on securing adequate drainage of the urinary tract, initially by placement of a bladder catheter and later by primary ablation of the valves. Vesicostomy may be required in infants who show persistent obstruction and impaired urinary drainage despite ablation. Upper tract diversion is less commonly required but may be considered in patients with markedly dilated, tortuous ureters and worsening renal function despite adequate bladder drainage. The long-term outcome for infants with PUV depends on the degree of associated renal dysplasia; ongoing care with both a pediatric nephrologist and a urologist is recommended.

### Disorders of the Bladder

Congenital disorders of the bladder include classic bladder exstrophy (exstrophy-epispadias complex) and a more severe disorder called cloacal exstrophy.56 These disorders occur because of the failure of the cloacal membrane to be reinforced by ingrowth of mesoderm during fetal development. The cloacal membrane is therefore subject to premature rupture, and depending on the extent and timing of rupture, either classic bladder exstrophy or cloacal exstrophy develops.

Bladder exstrophy is a rare congenital malformation resulting in a lower abdominal wall defect exposing an open bladder and urethra, a wide diastasis of the pubic symphysis, and an epispadic urethral opening. The incidence of bladder exstrophy is estimated to be 1 in 10,000 births with a male predisposition. Bladder exstrophy can be detected prenatally, with absence of bladder filling, low-set umbilicus,

widened pubic rami, small genitalia, and a lower abdominal mass that increases in size through pregnancy. At birth, the examination reveals an exposed bladder and posterior urethra on the anterior abdominal wall, with urine dripping from the ureteric orifices on the bladder surface. Bilateral inguinal hernias are usually present and pubic bones can be felt on both sides of the bladder. In boys, the penis appears shorter and the epispadic urethra covers the dorsum of the penis. In females, the clitoris is completely split next to the open urethra. Abnormalities of the kidneys are seen in a minority of patients. Treatment involves urologic closure of the bladder often with pelvic osteotomy by orthopedic surgery.

Cloacal exstrophy represents a more severe abdominal wall defect, which is one of the rarest urologic anomalies with an incidence of 1 in 200,000 to 400,000 births. It is commonly referred to as the omphalocele, exstrophy, imperforate anus, and spinal abnormalities (OEIS) complex. The examination shows ileocecal and bladder exstrophy with an associated omphalocele, imperforate anus, complete phallic separation, undescended testes in males, vaginal duplication in females, and pubic diastasis. Abnormalities of the upper urinary tract are present in approximately 50% of patients and can be associated with progressive CKD. Infants with cloacal exstrophy require immediate evaluation in a tertiary center with renal ultrasound, spinal and hip imaging, and preparation for early closure of the omphalocele, creation of a colostomy or ileostomy, and initial surgical closure of the bladder. Patients require long-term follow-up with a multidisciplinary team that includes a pediatric urologist, pediatric orthopedic surgeon, pediatric nephrologist (if renal dysplasia is present), and appropriate support team.

Oligohydramnios of Renal Origin

Oligohydramnios/anhydramnios may occur in cases of bilateral renal agenesis (BRA), severe bilateral renal dysplasia, or severe LUTO (see also Chapters 14 and 23). Lack of amniotic fluid early in gestation is detrimental to fetal pulmonary function, and it results in pulmonary hypoplasia and features of Potter sequence. Recently serial amnioinfusions have shown promise in treatment of cases of early pregnancy renal anhydramnios (EPRA) because of congenital BRA.54 There is currently an ongoing National Institutes of Health (NIH)-sponsored clinical trial, Renal Anhydramnios Fetal Therapy (RAFT), to further evaluate this intervention in select fetal therapy centers. Ethical considerations regarding amnioinfusions to patients with BRA who will be dependent on lifelong dialysis and transplant support are considerable.57 As discussed above, in select patients with LUTO/ PUV, vesicoamniotic shunting and fetal cystoscopy with valve ablation may be a prenatal treatment option.53

### Congenital Nephrotic Syndrome

Congenital nephrotic syndrome (CNS) is a rare condition defined as the development of nephrotic syndrome within the first 3 months of life.58 A prenatal diagnosis of CNS should be suspected in mothers with a high serum or amniotic fluid α-fetoprotein concentration, which results from massive fetal urinary protein losses. Placental edema, ascites, or fetal hydrops on prenatal ultrasonography should also raise suspicion of this disorder. Infants with CNS typically have full-blown nephrotic syndrome at birth or shortly afterward, with massive proteinuria, profound hypoalbuminemia, hyperlipidemia, and generalized edema. Infants with congenital nephrotic syndrome are often delivered prematurely and may be small for gestational age. The placenta is large, weighing greater than 25% of the infant’s birth weight.

CNS is most commonly caused by mutations in genes encoding proteins in the glomerular epithelial cell (podocyte), a cell critical in the prevention of urinary protein loss. The most common mutation is in NPHS1, a gene that encodes an important slit diaphragm protein called nephrin. Mutations in nephrin are most common in the Finnish population; thus infants with CNS because of nephrin mutations are said to have Finnish-type nephrotic syndrome. Other genetic mutations have been associated with CNS, including mutations in NPHS2 (podocin), LAMB2 (laminin β-2 gene), PLCE1 (phospholipase C epsilon gene) and WT-1 (Wilms tumor suppressor gene). Pathogenic variants in WT-1 can result in isolated kidney disease or developmental syndromes, including Denys-Drash and Frasier syndrome, both characterized by nephrotic syndrome, genital anomalies, pseudohermaphroditism, and risk for tumor development.

The evaluation and treatment of congenital nephrotic syndrome is complex. The clinician should first exclude nongenetic causes such as syphilis, HIV, CMV, hepatitis B, congenital rubella, toxoplasmosis, membranous nephropathy from maternal anti-NEP (neutral endopeptidase) antibodies, and maternal systemic lupus erythematosus, all of which have been associated with treatable forms of congenital nephrotic syndrome. If genetic CNS is suspected, testing by whole exome sequencing or next generation sequencing is recommended to establish an accurate diagnosis. Corticosteroids and immunosuppressive agents, used in treatment of nephrotic syndrome in older children, are not recommended. Current treatment includes intravenous albumin infusions, diuretics, aggressive nutritional support, thyroid hormone replacement, preventative anticoagulation, and prompt treatment of infectious and thromboembolic complications. ### Polycystic Kidney Disease

ARPKD is a rare inherited disorder characterized by enlarged cystic kidneys and a developmental bile duct disorder called congenital hepatic fibrosis.59 ARPKD occurs in approximately 1 in 20,000 live births. Prenatal ultrasonography shows enlarged, echogenic kidneys and oligohydramnios. The newborn infant with ARPKD may present with palpable abdominal masses, severe hypertension, and renal insufficiency. Respiratory failure related to pulmonary hypoplasia and abdominal distention from the massively enlarged kidneys is common. Although the majority of patients with ARPKD present in the newborn period, there is significant phenotypic heterogeneity, and presentations later in childhood may occur.

ARPKD is caused by mutations in PKHD1, a gene that encodes fibrocystin/polyductin, a large protein localized in the primary cilia of the kidney tubular cells, liver bile duct epithelia, and pancreas. The genetic defect leads to fusiform dilatation of the renal collecting ducts, causing enlarged echogenic kidneys without visible macrocysts. Virtually all infants with ARPKD have histologic evidence of congenital hepatic fibrosis at birth, although clinical evidence of hepatic involvement is present in only 40% of affected newborns.

The diagnosis of ARPKD is typically made based on characteristic clinical features, including enlarged echogenic kidneys, history of oligohydramnios, and hypertension. However, other cystic disorders, including diffuse cystic dysplasia, HNF-1B-related kidney disease, and ADPKD can be clinically indistinguishable from ARPKD in the newborn period. Although initial management is similar for all of the disorders, establishing a definitive diagnosis may be helpful for offering prognosis, surveillance for associated conditions, and future family planning. Therefore it is recommended to offer genetic testing via next generation sequencing cystic kidney disease panel.60

The management of the neonate with ARPKD is largely supportive.61 Mechanical ventilation, including oscillator support, is critical in neonates with respiratory failure because of pulmonary hypoplasia or respiratory embarrassment from abdominal distension. Infants with hypertension may require the administration of multiple antihypertensive agents to achieve blood pressure control. Patients may require correction of electrolyte imbalances, notably hyponatremia. Optimizing nutrition may be challenging owing to massively enlarged kidneys causing feeding difficulties. Unilateral nephrectomies have not been shown to improve the respiratory status but may improve feeding issues. Hepatic complications, such as portal hypertension, are typically absent in the newborn period, and synthetic function is intact.

aggressive ventilatory support. Long-term survival has improved, and approximately 80% of neonatal survivors are alive at 10 years of life. Morbidity is significant, however, and complications include CKD, growth failure, hepatic complications (portal hypertension, bleeding varices, and ascending cholangitis), and chronic lung disease.59 Progression to end-stage kidney disease requiring dialysis or kidney transplantation occurs in 50% of patients in the first decade of life. With improving long-term survival, portal hypertension has emerged as an important cause of morbidity and mortality, and subsequent liver transplantation may be necessary.

Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) caused by mutations in PKD1 and PKD2 genes may also present in the neonatal period. Clinical presentation ranges from asymptomatic cysts on renal ultrasonography to severe neonatal manifestations similar to that of ARPKD. Because the two conditions can be very similar, a careful family history and genetic testing is recommended to establish the correct diagnosis.60 Although ADPKD is a systemic disease that commonly affects the liver, gastrointestinal tract, and cerebral vessels in adults, such extra-renal manifestations are rarely seen until adulthood.

### Neonatal Bartter Syndrome

Neonatal Bartter syndrome is a rare autosomal recessive hypokalemic tubulopathy with an incidence of 1 in 50,000 to 100,000 live births. Newborn infants with neonatal Bartter syndrome typically present with profound salt wasting, polyuria, hypokalemia, and hypercalciuria (see also Chapter 96).

### Renal Tubular Acidosis

RTA is caused by a defect in the reabsorption of bicarbonate or in the secretion of hydrogen ions that is not related to a decrease in GFR. RTA should be suspected in infants with persistent hyperchloremic (i.e., nonanion gap) metabolic acidosis. There are three forms of RTA: proximal (type II), distal (type I), and hyperkalemic (type IV). RTA may be sporadic or inherited as a classic Mendelian trait. RTA is discussed in detail in Chapter 96.

### Tumors of the Kidney

Renal tumors in the newborn infant are uncommon, accounting for approximately 5% to 7% of all neonatal tumors.62 Because only 15% of renal tumors are detected antenatally, the most common neonatal clinical presentation is abdominal distension or a palpable mass. Congenital mesoblastic nephroma (nephroblastoma) is the most common renal tumor in the first year of life, accounting for 54% to 66% of tumors.62 The clinical presentation is usually a unilateral abdominal mass, although congestive heart failure and hypertension are less common manifestations. Seventy percent of affected pregnancies are associated with polyhydramnios; premature delivery and hydrops have been reported. Multilocular cystic nephroma is a benign renal tumor that consists of large cysts separated by fibrous septae. This tumor arises from metanephric blastema and has features of immature blastema, tubules, and skeletal muscle on histopathologic examination. The prognosis is excellent with nephrectomy alone.

Wilms tumor accounts for 20% of all renal tumors diagnosed in the neonatal population. The clinical presentation is similar to that of congenital mesoblastic nephroma. Most Wilms tumors occur sporadically, but 10% are associated with congenital syndromes that predispose to tumorigenesis, including WAGR (Wilms tumor, aniridia, genitourinary anomalies, range of developmental delays) syndrome, Denys-Drash syndrome, Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome, and Golabi-Behmel syndrome.62

Malignant rhabdoid tumor of the kidney (MRTK) is a rare but very aggressive renal tumor, often presenting with metastatic disease. It is treated with nephrectomy, chemotherapy, and radiation therapy and has a poor outcome. Clear cell sarcoma of the kidney (CCTK) is the least common neonatal renal tumor, accounting for only 3% of renal tumors in this age group. Treatment is similar to that of MRTK, with nephrectomy, chemotherapy, and radiation therapy; patient outcome is less dismal than with neonatal MRTK.

Definitive therapy for a localized renal mass begins with surgical exploration, and in most cases the primary treatment is nephrectomy. Infants with congenital mesoblastic nephromas and multilocular cystic nephromas require no postoperative therapy. Infants with malignant tumors such as Wilms tumor are treated according to well-established oncologic protocols. The prognosis for most infants with neonatal renal tumors is good, although an infant with aggressive Wilms tumor, atypical congenital mesoblastic nephroma, MRTK, or CCTK may have a poor prognosis.
