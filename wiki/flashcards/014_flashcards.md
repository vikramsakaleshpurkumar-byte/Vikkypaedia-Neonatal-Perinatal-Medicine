<details><summary>How do SGA and fetal growth restriction differ?</summary>

SGA describes size and does not by itself prove FGR. SGA is a size category; constitutionally small infants can be SGA, and FGR can occur above the tenth percentile.

PDF p. 276
</details>

<details><summary>What common pathway causes fetal growth restriction?</summary>

Hypoxia and nutrient deprivation from placental insufficiency. Placental insufficiency limits fetal oxygen and nutrient delivery and is the commonest stated cause.

PDF p. 276
</details>

<details><summary>What anthropometric pattern characterizes head-sparing FGR?</summary>

Asymmetric head-sparing restriction. Redistribution conserves delivery to brain and heart, producing relatively preserved head size and reduced weight or abdominal size.

PDF p. 278
</details>

<details><summary>What information helps evaluate suspected growth restriction after birth?</summary>

Pregnancy trajectory, anthropometry, examination and placental findings. Prenatal pattern, maternal and family factors, physical findings and placental pathology can reveal the cause.

PDF p. 283
</details>

<details><summary>Why are growth-restricted newborns vulnerable to hypoglycemia?</summary>

Limited glycogen and fat reserves with impaired gluconeogenesis. Reduced stores, impaired gluconeogenesis and sometimes limited ketone production increase vulnerability.

PDF p. 284
</details>

<details><summary>Does fetal growth restriction guarantee accelerated functional lung maturity?</summary>

No. The source describes reduced lung growth, altered alveolar structure and pulmonary vascular dysfunction, with increased respiratory morbidity despite proposed stress-related maturation effects.

PDF p. 285
</details>
