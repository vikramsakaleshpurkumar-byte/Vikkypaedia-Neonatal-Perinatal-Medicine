<details><summary>What should the immediate delivery-room newborn survey assess?</summary>

Breathing, color and a brief whole-body search for major anomalies or injury. The primary survey checks transition, major anomalies and birth injuries across the body.

PDF p. 467
</details>

<details><summary>When should the detailed routine newborn examination occur in a well infant?</summary>

Perform the detailed examination within 24 hours while allowing the initial contact and feed. The source recommends routine examination within 24 hours while protecting at least the first hour and initial breastfeeding in well dyads.

PDF p. 467
</details>

<details><summary>How can examination order be adapted when a newborn is quiet?</summary>

Listen to the heart and perform suitable quiet-state assessments while ensuring eventual whole-body examination. The sequence is flexible; heart, eyes, pulses and gentle hip assessment can take advantage of a quiet state.

PDF p. 468
</details>

<details><summary>What should follow an abnormal newborn red reflex?</summary>

Direct ophthalmology consultation. An abnormal reflex can indicate serious ocular disease and warrants direct ophthalmologic assessment.

PDF p. 473
</details>

<details><summary>Why can duct-dependent heart disease escape the first examination?</summary>

A patent ductus and high right-sided pressure can mask important disease. Hemodynamic transition may delay murmurs or symptoms until ductal closure and pressure changes occur.

PDF p. 475
</details>

<details><summary>How should newborn growth percentiles be interpreted?</summary>

Use gestation-specific charts at birth; charts representing40-week newborns can misclassify late-preterm or postmature infants.

</details>
