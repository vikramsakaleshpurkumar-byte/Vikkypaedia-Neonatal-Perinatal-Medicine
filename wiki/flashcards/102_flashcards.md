<details><summary>Can a neonate with bone or joint infection lack prominent fever or leukocytosis?</summary>

Skeletal infection remains possible despite subtle systemic findings. Immature neonatal responses may produce subtle signs; reduced movement, feeding intolerance or irritability can be early clues.

<details><summary>Why does hematogenous osteomyelitis commonly begin near the metaphysis?</summary>

Vascular loops and sluggish metaphyseal flow favor bacterial lodging. The metaphyseal vascular arrangement promotes trapping and proliferation during hematogenous spread.

<details><summary>Which imaging study is particularly sensitive for early osteomyelitis and its extent?</summary>

Magnetic resonance imaging. MRI is described as sensitive for early infection and anatomic extent; stability and transfer or sedation needs affect feasibility.

<details><summary>What procedure is required for definitive investigation of suspected septic arthritis?</summary>

Joint aspiration by an experienced clinician. Aspiration permits culture, Gram stain and synovial fluid assessment; blood cultures are also obtained.

</details>

<details><summary>Why does neonatal septic arthritis require urgent drainage and antimicrobial treatment?</summary>

Cartilage damage and pressure-related compromise of epiphyseal blood supply. Pus and inflammation damage cartilage; increased pressure can compromise epiphyseal perfusion and cause permanent injury.

PDF p. 2075
</details>

<details><summary>Why can neonatal osteomyelitis extend into an adjacent joint at certain sites?</summary>

Some metaphyses are intra-articular, and neonatal vascular anatomy permits spread; proximal femur, proximal humerus, proximal radius and distal tibia are described examples.
