<details><summary>Why is effective ventilation central in neonatal cardiovascular collapse?</summary>

Asphyxia from inadequate gas exchange. Asphyxia is the usual cause; establishing ventilation often avoids compressions and drugs.

</details>

<details><summary>When are neonatal chest compressions indicated in the described algorithm?</summary>

Chest compressions coordinated with ventilation. Persistent severe bradycardia despite effective lung inflation is the compression indication described.

PDF p. 582
</details>

<details><summary>How does epinephrine support coronary perfusion during neonatal CPR?</summary>

Raise aortic diastolic pressure to improve coronary perfusion. Coronary perfusion during CPR depends on the diastolic pressure gradient; epinephrine can improve that gradient.

PDF p. 585
</details>

<details><summary>Does shock after asphyxia necessarily indicate hypovolemia?</summary>

Myocardial dysfunction may cause shock without low circulating volume. Most asphyxiated infants are not hypovolemic; reduced myocardial function and cardiac output can explain shock.

PDF p. 585
</details>

<details><summary>What diagnosis should be considered after sudden deterioration during initially effective ventilation?</summary>

Pneumothorax impairing venous return. A large pneumothorax can compress venous return, reduce output and cause hypotension and bradycardia.

PDF p. 590
</details>

<details><summary>Why should complex resuscitations be documented and debriefed?</summary>

Accurate event recording and deliberate team discussion identify improvements in preparation, communication and technical performance for rare high-stakes events.
