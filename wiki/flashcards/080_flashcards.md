<details><summary>Why should neonatal coagulation results use age-appropriate reference ranges?</summary>

Developmental hemostasis differs from adults and requires neonatal interpretation. Several procoagulants and anticoagulants are lower in neonates; counterbalancing differences and age-specific ranges matter.

</details>

<details><summary>What is the mechanism of fetal and neonatal alloimmune thrombocytopenia?</summary>

Maternal IgG targeting fetal platelets bearing a paternal antigen. Transplacental maternal alloantibodies bind incompatible fetal platelet antigens and promote platelet clearance.

PDF p. 1503
</details>

<details><summary>Which severe inherited factor deficiency may have normal PT and aPTT?</summary>

Factor XIII deficiency. Factor XIII deficiency is an exception in which routine PT and aPTT can remain normal; specific quantitative assays are needed.

PDF p. 1510
</details>

<details><summary>Which screening test usually becomes abnormal first in vitamin K deficiency?</summary>

Isolated PT prolongation before aPTT prolongation. PT prolongation is the earliest screening abnormality, with aPTT prolongation as deficiency progresses.

PDF p. 1511
</details>

<details><summary>How can disseminated intravascular coagulation produce both bleeding and organ injury?</summary>

Systemic coagulation activation with factor consumption and microvascular thrombosis. DIC combines coagulation and fibrinolytic activation, consumption of platelets and factors, and fibrin deposition that may impair organs.

PDF p. 1511
</details>

<details><summary>Which triad suggests neonatal renal vein thrombosis?</summary>

Hematuria, a palpable flank mass and thrombocytopenia are cardinal clues; not every infant has all three, and Doppler ultrasonography confirms the diagnosis.

PDF p. 1513
</details>
