<details><summary>Why is newborn screening important for congenital hypothyroidism?</summary>

Early detection and treatment can prevent neurodevelopmental disability. The chapter emphasizes prevention of neurodevelopmental consequences through early recognition and treatment.

</details>

<details><summary>Why can TSH-only screening miss central hypothyroidism?</summary>

Pituitary or hypothalamic deficiency may not produce high TSH. Central hypothyroidism can have normal or only mildly elevated TSH; T4-based assessment is needed when suspected.

PDF p. 1734
</details>

<details><summary>What is the source principle on timing replacement for confirmed or strongly suspected congenital hypothyroidism?</summary>

Start replacement promptly rather than delaying for a complete etiologic workup. Treatment should begin promptly after confirmatory sampling; maternal T4 protection is temporary and delays can harm development.

PDF p. 1739
</details>

<details><summary>Can neonatal Graves disease occur after maternal thyroidectomy or radioiodine treatment?</summary>

Persistent maternal stimulating TSH-receptor antibodies crossing the placenta. Maternal receptor-stimulating antibodies may persist after treatment and cross the placenta.

PDF p. 1741
</details>

<details><summary>How can a thyroxine-binding globulin defect affect thyroid tests?</summary>

An inherited thyroid-hormone binding protein abnormality. TBG excess or deficiency can change total hormone concentrations while free T4 remains normal.

PDF p. 1743
</details>

<details><summary>Why must neonatal TSH and free T4 be interpreted using age-appropriate values?</summary>

The neonatal thyroid axis changes after birth; developmental reference intervals and the clinical context prevent inappropriate interpretation using adult values.

PDF p. 1728
</details>
