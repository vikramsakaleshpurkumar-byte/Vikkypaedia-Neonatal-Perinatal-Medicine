<details><summary>What are the four modes of neonatal heat exchange?</summary>

Convection, radiation, evaporation and conduction. All four heat-exchange modes vary with maturity, environment and postnatal age.

</details>

<details><summary>Why is evaporative heat loss especially high in extremely preterm infants?</summary>

An immature skin barrier. Skin barrier competence develops with maturity, so very immature infants have much higher transepidermal water and heat loss.

PDF p. 605
</details>

<details><summary>What defines the thermoneutral zone?</summary>

The range allowing normal temperature at minimal metabolic rate using nonevaporative adjustments. Thermoneutrality includes metabolic efficiency; a normal temperature alone does not prove it.

PDF p. 607
</details>

<details><summary>How does increased ambient humidity affect evaporative heat loss?</summary>

Increase appropriate incubator humidity. Higher humidity lowers evaporative loss and can aid fluid and heat balance in very immature infants.

PDF p. 610
</details>

<details><summary>What thermal hazard can follow skin-servo probe detachment?</summary>

Overwarming from inappropriate heat delivery. A detached probe can lead to overwarming; device readings must be interpreted alongside infant assessment.

PDF p. 610
</details>

<details><summary>How does skin maturation change thermal requirements after very preterm birth?</summary>

As the barrier matures over days and weeks, evaporative loss falls, so the environment required for thermal balance changes and should be reassessed.

</details>
