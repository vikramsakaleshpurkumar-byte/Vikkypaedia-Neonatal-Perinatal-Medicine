<details><summary>Does absence of vesicles exclude disseminated neonatal HSV?</summary>

Disseminated herpes simplex infection. Disseminated HSV can involve liver and lungs and may occur without skin vesicles.

<details><summary>Why is CSF HSV PCR useful?</summary>

CSF HSV PCR. PCR is especially valuable because CSF viral culture has low recovery; blood PCR alone does not classify disease extent.

</details>

<details><summary>By what age must CMV be identified to establish congenital infection in this source?</summary>

Identification within the first 21 days. Early identification is necessary because later positivity may reflect perinatal acquisition.

</details>

<details><summary>What birth intervention does the source describe for an infant of an HBsAg-positive mother?</summary>

Hepatitis B vaccine plus HBIG at separate sites within 12 hours. Active vaccine and passive immunoglobulin provide complementary protection after known maternal infection.

PDF p. 958
</details>

<details><summary>How can parvovirus B19 produce fetal hydrops?</summary>

Infection of erythroid precursors causing impaired red-cell production. Erythroid maturation arrest can cause profound anemia and high-output cardiac failure with hydrops.

PDF p. 961
</details>

<details><summary>Why can HSV serology be difficult to interpret in newborns?</summary>

Transplacentally transferred maternal antibodies confound interpretation; virologic tests from appropriate sites are more useful for neonatal diagnosis.
