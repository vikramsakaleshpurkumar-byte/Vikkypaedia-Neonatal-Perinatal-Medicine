<details><summary>How do structured and unstructured neonatal data differ?</summary>

Coded gestational age: structured; free-text note: unstructured. Structured data use predefined fields or coding; free text, pictures and audio are examples of unstructured information.

<details><summary>What must happen before a large neonatal dataset can reliably inform care?</summary>

Curate, organize, explore and validate the data. The chapter emphasizes preparation, accessibility and validation; a large volume alone does not establish usefulness.

<details><summary>What is ascertainment bias in neonatal database research?</summary>

Ascertainment bias from changed identification. The chapter uses this example to show how changing recognition and classification can produce an apparent incidence increase.

PDF p. 50
</details>

<details><summary>When can neonatal database studies be particularly useful?</summary>

Generate and investigate hypotheses while addressing bias. Large observational datasets can investigate questions difficult to randomize, but their design and bias limitations remain.

PDF p. 49
</details>

<details><summary>How can neonatal databases support quality improvement?</summary>

Consistent process and outcome definitions. Pre-existing, consistent measures allow services to track change and collaborate on quality improvement.

PDF p. 50
</details>

<details><summary>Does a large database replace a prospective randomized trial?</summary>

No. It can reveal associations, rare events and care patterns and generate hypotheses, but confounding, classification and other biases remain important.
