<details><summary>How does developmental timing distinguish malformations from deformations in the source?</summary>

Deformation of an essentially formed part. Early embryonic disturbances produce malformations; later fetal influences can alter the configuration of essentially normal structures.

</details>

<details><summary>Which circumstances can increase fetal constraint and positional deformity?</summary>

Increased uterine compression affecting fetal position and motion. Oligohydramnios and other constraint factors may produce contractures and angular or torsional changes; some resolve, others require care.

</details>

<details><summary>What posture is characteristic of upper brachial plexus injury?</summary>

Upper brachial plexus palsy. The described waiter’s-tip posture is characteristic of the common upper plexus injury pattern.

PDF p. 2068
</details>

<details><summary>Why should clavicular fracture be considered in a newborn with reduced arm movement?</summary>

Pain-related pseudoparalysis from fracture. Clavicular fracture can cause painful reduced motion and mimic a neurologic palsy; examination and imaging distinguish causes.

PDF p. 2069
</details>

<details><summary>Why can routine radiographs miss proximal humeral epiphyseal separation at birth?</summary>

The proximal humeral epiphysis is not yet ossified. An unossified epiphysis can conceal separation on plain radiography; ultrasound or other imaging and later callus may help.

PDF p. 2070
</details>

<details><summary>Why must infection remain in the differential of a newborn with decreased active limb movement?</summary>

Septic arthritis and osteomyelitis can also cause painful reduced movement, so delivery trauma or nerve injury should not be assumed from pseudoparalysis alone.

</details>
