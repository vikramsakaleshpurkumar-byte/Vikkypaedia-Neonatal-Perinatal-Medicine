<details><summary>What resuscitation capability should be present at every delivery in the source?</summary>

An individual responsible for the infant and capable of initiating PPV. Unexpected neonatal depression occurs; the source calls for a trained person whose primary responsibility is the infant.

</details>

<details><summary>Which organ takes over primary gas exchange at birth?</summary>

Aerated lungs with increased pulmonary perfusion. The lungs replace the placenta as the primary gas-exchange organ and must receive pulmonary blood flow.

PDF p. 545
</details>

<details><summary>Why should indicated resuscitation begin before the one-minute Apgar score?</summary>

Begin indicated support based on breathing and heart rate without waiting for Apgar scoring. Waiting for the score delays therapy; Apgar describes the infant’s state at specified times.

PDF p. 551
</details>

<details><summary>Does a vigorous infant born through clear fluid require routine suctioning?</summary>

No routine suctioning is necessary. Routine suction is unnecessary in this setting; posterior pharyngeal stimulation may provoke bradycardia.

PDF p. 554
</details>

<details><summary>What should follow initial stimulation if the infant remains apneic?</summary>

Prompt positive-pressure ventilation. Failure to respond should prompt PPV; ongoing ineffective stimulation delays ventilation and may worsen hypoxia.

PDF p. 554
</details>

<details><summary>Why are very preterm infants at high risk of heat loss during initial stabilization?</summary>

They have a high surface-area-to-mass ratio, limited brown fat and immature thermal defenses; preplanned thermal measures and temperature monitoring are needed.

</details>
