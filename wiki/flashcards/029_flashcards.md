<details><summary>Does congenital mean genetic?</summary>

The feature is present at birth, with genetic or nongenetic causes possible. Congenital denotes presence at birth; it does not specify cause.

PDF p. 518
</details>

<details><summary>What distinguishes a major anomaly from a minor anomaly in the source?</summary>

Major anomaly. Major anomalies have significant surgical or cosmetic implications; minor anomalies do not have that significance.

PDF p. 518
</details>

<details><summary>Why should multiple minor anomalies prompt a careful pattern search?</summary>

Look for a characteristic pattern and associated major malformations. Minor findings may be normal variants but combinations can identify patterns or associated major anomalies.

PDF p. 523
</details>

<details><summary>What family-history approach supports evaluation of congenital anomalies?</summary>

A detailed three- to four-generation pedigree including losses and consanguinity. The source recommends a multigeneration pedigree with affected relatives, pregnancy losses and possible parental relatedness.

PDF p. 525
</details>

<details><summary>Why can parental samples help interpret a neonatal copy-number variant?</summary>

Whether it is de novo or inherited, alongside phenotype assessment. Microarray can identify variants not responsible for the phenotype; inheritance information aids interpretation.

PDF p. 538
</details>

<details><summary>What prenatal information should be reviewed in a newborn anomaly evaluation?</summary>

Pregnancy course, timed maternal illnesses and exposures, fetal movement and prenatal ultrasound, screening and diagnostic genetic results.

</details>
