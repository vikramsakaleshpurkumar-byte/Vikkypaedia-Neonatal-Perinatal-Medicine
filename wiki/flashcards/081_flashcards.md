<details><summary>Why does erythropoietin fall after birth?</summary>

Higher tissue oxygenation suppresses erythropoietin production. Improved postnatal oxygenation lowers EPO and erythropoiesis, contributing to physiologic anemia.

</details>

<details><summary>Which initial tests help classify neonatal anemia?</summary>

CBC with indices, reticulocyte count and peripheral smear. Indices, reticulocytes and morphology guide the differential alongside history and examination.

PDF p. 1526
</details>

<details><summary>What findings commonly support hemolysis rather than uncomplicated blood loss?</summary>

Accelerated red-cell destruction. Hemolysis commonly increases indirect bilirubin and LDH and stimulates reticulocytes; concurrent marrow suppression can blunt that response.

PDF p. 1530
</details>

<details><summary>Why can maternal elevation of Hb F affect the Kleihauer-Betke test?</summary>

Maternal Hb F-containing cells can produce a false-positive result. The assay exploits acid resistance of Hb F-containing cells, so elevated maternal Hb F can confound results; flow cytometry may distinguish populations.

PDF p. 1530
</details>

<details><summary>Why should specimen source be considered when assessing neonatal polycythemia?</summary>

Capillary values tend to exceed peripheral venous values. The chapter defines term polycythemia using peripheral venous values and notes higher capillary values; viscosity and clinical manifestations also matter.

PDF p. 1548
</details>

<details><summary>Why does anemia of prematurity reach an earlier and often lower nadir than physiologic anemia in term infants?</summary>

Premature red cells have shorter survival and EPO remains low; illness and phlebotomy can add further losses or suppress production.

</details>
