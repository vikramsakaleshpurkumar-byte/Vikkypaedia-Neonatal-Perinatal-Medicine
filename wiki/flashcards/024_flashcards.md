<details><summary>What is the most common route to intra-amniotic infection?</summary>

Ascension from the lower maternal genital tract. Most cases result from ascending bacteria; procedural and hematogenous routes occur less often.

</details>

<details><summary>Does negative amniotic fluid culture exclude all intra-amniotic inflammation?</summary>

Fastidious microbes or sterile intra-amniotic inflammation. Negative cultures may reflect recovery limitations or sterile inflammation, so inflammation and infection are not identical labels.

PDF p. 429
</details>

<details><summary>What limitation affects clinical diagnostic criteria for chorioamnionitis?</summary>

Individual signs have variable sensitivity and generally low specificity. Clinical criteria are often used because definitive sampling is unavailable, but their specificity is limited.

PDF p. 430
</details>

<details><summary>Is chorioamnionitis alone a sufficient indication for cesarean delivery?</summary>

Infection alone does not justify cesarean delivery. No neonatal benefit from cesarean solely for chorioamnionitis is established, and maternal surgical complications may increase.

PDF p. 434
</details>

<details><summary>Why should the obstetric fever assessment be communicated to neonatology?</summary>

The diagnosis, level of suspicion and rationale for maternal management. The source calls for conveying the diagnosis and management rationale so neonatal decisions reflect actual suspicion.

PDF p. 434
</details>

<details><summary>Why should outcomes attributed to chorioamnionitis be interpreted cautiously?</summary>

Maternal fever has multiple causes, and clinical infection, microbiologic findings and histologic inflammation do not perfectly agree.
