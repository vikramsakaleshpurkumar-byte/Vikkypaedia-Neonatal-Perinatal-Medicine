<details><summary>Which fraction of circulating calcium is physiologically active?</summary>

Ionized calcium. Ionized calcium is the active fraction; total calcium also includes protein-bound and complexed fractions.

</details>

<details><summary>How can a high phosphate load contribute to late neonatal hypocalcemia?</summary>

Limited renal phosphorus excretion and immature response to PTH. Immature renal handling and relative PTH resistance can promote phosphate retention and lower calcium.

PDF p. 1709
</details>

<details><summary>Why should magnesium be considered in an infant with hypocalcemia?</summary>

Hypomagnesemia can coexist with impaired parathyroid function and hypocalcemia. Magnesium deficiency may impair PTH-related calcium regulation; the source describes improvement in calcium and PTH with correction in relevant infants.

PDF p. 1716
</details>

<details><summary>Which maternal treatment can produce neonatal hypermagnesemia?</summary>

Magnesium sulfate. Maternal magnesium sulfate can raise fetal and neonatal magnesium; renal function affects clearance.

PDF p. 1718
</details>

<details><summary>How does early birth increase the risk of metabolic bone disease?</summary>

Loss of late-gestation calcium and phosphorus accretion. Premature delivery interrupts fetal mineral accretion; postnatal nutrition and illness add to risk.

PDF p. 1719
</details>

<details><summary>Why may tremors or seizures fail to distinguish hypocalcemia from hypomagnesemia?</summary>

Their neuromuscular manifestations overlap, and the deficiencies can coexist; laboratory assessment is needed rather than symptom labeling alone.
