<details><summary>What should be considered before classifying hypotonia as a primary neuromuscular disorder?</summary>

Assess acute systemic illness, metabolic disturbance and medication exposure. Many acute or multisystem illnesses and maternal medications can reduce tone.

</details>

<details><summary>What general pattern favors a central cause of hypotonia?</summary>

Central hypotonia with less weakness than the degree of low tone. Central causes often reduce tone disproportionately to weakness; exceptions require full assessment.

PDF p. 1121
</details>

<details><summary>What pattern of weakness is described in infantile spinal muscular atrophy?</summary>

Anterior horn cell degeneration. Infantile SMA affects spinal anterior horn cells and lower brainstem motor nuclei.

PDF p. 1123
</details>

<details><summary>Which maternal history can clarify severe neonatal weakness with facial diplegia?</summary>

Congenital myotonic dystrophy. The congenital form of DM1 is usually maternally inherited; maternal examination and molecular testing support diagnosis.

PDF p. 1130
</details>

<details><summary>What enzyme defect causes infantile Pompe disease?</summary>

Acid alpha-glucosidase. Acid alpha-glucosidase deficiency produces lysosomal glycogen accumulation involving muscle and heart.

PDF p. 1133
</details>

<details><summary>Why does absence of clinical myotonia not exclude congenital myotonic dystrophy?</summary>

Neonatal clinical and electrical myotonia may be absent and develop later; severe weakness, facial features and maternal findings can be more informative at birth.
