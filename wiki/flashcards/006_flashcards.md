<details><summary>What is the patient-safety rationale for simulation?</summary>

Repeated practice in a safe simulated setting. Simulation permits repeated practice and remediation before patient exposure, consistent with avoiding preventable harm.

PDF p. 119
</details>

<details><summary>Why does a fixed training duration not establish competence?</summary>

Judge demonstrated performance rather than attendance alone. Learners have different experience, strengths and learning rates; time spent does not by itself show successful performance.

PDF p. 119
</details>

<details><summary>How does debriefing differ from briefing and one-way feedback?</summary>

Debriefing. Debriefing discusses completed events through interaction; briefing concerns planned events, and feedback can flow one way.

PDF p. 122
</details>

<details><summary>What additional safety benefit can in situ simulation provide?</summary>

Identification of a latent safety threat. In situ simulation can uncover equipment, personnel and communication problems in the actual clinical environment.

PDF p. 123
</details>

<details><summary>What does Helping Babies Breathe illustrate about fidelity?</summary>

Appropriate low-fidelity simulation can be effective. Helping Babies Breathe is described as a successful low-fidelity approach adapted to resource-limited settings.

PDF p. 122
</details>

<details><summary>What should a simulation program do when weaknesses become apparent?</summary>

Identify and remediate them through suitable practice, feedback and, where appropriate, debriefing rather than treating participation alone as competence.

PDF p. 119
</details>
