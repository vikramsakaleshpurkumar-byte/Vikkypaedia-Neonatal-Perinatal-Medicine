<details><summary>Does the absence of a family history exclude an inborn error of metabolism?</summary>

An inherited metabolic disorder remains possible. Most affected neonates may lack similarly affected relatives; recessive inheritance and new variants help explain this.

</details>

<details><summary>Can documented infection exclude an underlying metabolic disorder?</summary>

Continue it because infection and metabolic disease can coexist. The chapter specifically notes that metabolic disorders can predispose to sepsis, including galactosemia with E. coli infection.

PDF p. 1787
</details>

<details><summary>Which initial blood tests help classify a suspected neonatal metabolic disorder?</summary>

Glucose, blood gas, electrolytes, lactate, ketones and ammonia. The source core screening set uses these tests to identify glucose, acid-base, energy and nitrogen-disposal disturbances.

PDF p. 1832
</details>

<details><summary>What is the most effective general approach to metabolic acidosis?</summary>

Identify and treat the cause while supporting physiology. The source emphasizes cause-directed treatment and assessment of common systemic causes as well as inborn errors.

PDF p. 1840
</details>

<details><summary>What clinical pattern should prompt immediate evaluation for hyperammonemia?</summary>

Plasma ammonia with appropriate specimen handling. Hyperammonemia may progress rapidly to coma and irreversible neurologic injury; immediate investigation is essential.

PDF p. 1856
</details>

<details><summary>Why must collection and handling instructions be established before specialized metabolic samples are obtained?</summary>

Preanalytic requirements affect whether results are interpretable; rapid screening and specialist tests require reliable sampling rather than indiscriminate collection.
