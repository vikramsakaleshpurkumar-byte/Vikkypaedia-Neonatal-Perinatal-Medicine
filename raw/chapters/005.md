5 Improving the Quality, Safety, and Equity of Neonatal Intensive Care for Infants and Families

### JOCHEN PROFIT, JEFFREY B. GOULD, MICHELLE-MARIE PEÑA, KIMBERLY NOVOD, LINDA S. FRANCK, DANIELLE E.Y. EHRET, AND JEFFREY D. HORBAR

### Overview

Over the last decades, neonatology has achieved dramatic decreases in neonatal mortality and morbidity globally. Unfortunately, wide variation persists across neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) with respect to the quality of care and outcomes, as well as the extent of medical errors. Racial and ethnic disparities are also a consideration with regard to quality of care and outcomes. An important goal of quality improvement is to reduce variation in outcomes and errors and to address disparities in order to ensure the best possible outcomes for infants and their families. In neonatal quality improvement, we assess the need for action based on quantitative outcomes data and qualitative input from NICU staff and families. The location and type of intervention are determined by an analysis of the context and processes believed to drive desired change. The run chart displays data over time and indicates if those interventions have been successful. This chapter presents the historical development of neonatology quality improvement and provides an overview of models, methods, and measures of NICU-based quality improvement. We review the ways data can be collected, evaluated, and applied to improve the quality, safety, and equity of medical care for newborn infants and their families.

Making improvements happen also requires ... unshakeable belief in the idea that everyone in healthcare really has two jobs when they come to work every day: to do their work and to improve it.1

Systematic evaluation of the quality, safety, and efficiency of clinical care has become an integral part of medical practice. Health professionals, hospitals, and large health

care organizations are required to monitor, report on, and continuously improve their services. Public release of hospital performance data is becoming increasingly common. Health professionals in neonatology must learn how to evaluate themselves and learn how they will be evaluated by others, including policy makers, hospital administrators, regulators, payers, and the families and public they serve.

Evaluation is not an end in itself. Health professionals must learn how to use available information to improve the quality and safety of health care continuously. This is not just an option. It is a requirement set forth by regulatory licensing bodies (www.ABP.org) and a responsibility we have to patients and their families.

### The Case for Improvement

Public health and health care delivery systems in highincome nations have much to be proud of. Over the past century, they have combined to reduce infant and maternal mortality, as well as to prolong life expectancy to unsurpassed levels in human history. During the 20th century, infant mortality dropped from more than 100 per 1000 live births to about 5 per 1000 live births; maternal deaths dropped by 99% to about 10 per 100,000 live births.2–4

During the first half of the 20th century, these improvements were driven largely by advances in public health, especially access to clean water, which dramatically reduced infection-related morbidity and mortality.5 However, although health care delivery conferred little benefit to population-based health during the first half of the century, there have since been demonstrable contributions to added life expectancy.6 Neonatal intensive care is a case in point. Although relatively few interventions (e.g., heated incubator

On the other hand, new medical treatments in combination with greater access and demand for care and a rise in the prevalence of chronic disease conditions have resulted in rising health expenditures, which have outpaced national incomes.10,11 As a consequence, in the United States, health care consumes an ever-greater proportion of gross domestic product. In 2020, national health expenditures accounted for 18% of gross domestic product, which severely strains the budgets of those paying for health care: consumers, through taxes, co-pays, deductibles, insurance, and reduced wage growth; companies, through insurance; and state and federal governments, through obligations under Medicaid and Medicare.

Further, health care access and resources are not distributed equally among populations, creating disparate health and financial burdens for minoritized groups. In light of these constraints, a commitment to population health requires optimization of the value of each dollar expended for health care equitably. However, a sequence of sentinel Institute of Medicine of the National Academy of Sciences reports have highlighted that health care delivery, including neonatal intensive care, falls short of its value and equity potential,12 and they have provided a blueprint toward optimization using quality improvement and safety tools.13

In neonatology, as in other clinical fields, opportunities for improving the quality, safety, and equity of care are substantial. A compelling body of literature suggests that neonatal intensive care is supply-side sensitive14; that is, the more NICUs there are, the more patients will be admitted to them, potentially indicating waste. In addition, dramatic variation in the processes and outcomes of care has been documented that cannot be explained by differences in case mix, suggesting differences in the quality-of-care delivery.15–20 For example, in a study of over 400,000 infants with birth weights of 401 to 1500 grams born from 2005 to 2014 and cared for at 756 Vermont Oxford Network (VON) member NICUs in the United States, NICUs in the worst decile had rates nearly twice as high as those in the best decile for several major morbidities (late-onset infection, severe retinopathy of prematurity, severe intraventricular hemorrhage, and necrotizing enterocolitis) of very low birth weight (VLBW) infants.

Despite the variation, improvements in NICU care over the past decade have heralded dramatic improvement in mortality and morbidities for VLBW infants.19 Health care–associated infection rates provide a striking example. At the 756 US NICUs in the study described above, the rate of late bacterial (including coagulase-negative Staphylococcus) or fungal infection significantly decreased from 2005, when 21.9% of infants had infections, to 2014 when 10.1%

of the infants had infections. Remarkably, 75% of NICUs achieved rates of performance as good as or better than what the best 25% of units had achieved only 8 years earlier. Over the last decade, reduction of health care–associated infection has been at the center of national health policy efforts to improve the value of health care delivery, including aligning hospital payments with quality-of-care delivery. Widespread efforts in the neonatal community have proved successful in facilitating broad-based and sustained reductions in health care–associated infection rates and represent an encouraging blueprint for improvements in care in other areas.7 Such improvements have also resulted in improved survival without major morbidity. In a study of 49,333 VLBW infants (<1500 grams) across 142 NICUs in the California Perinatal Quality Care Collaborative (CPQCC) from 2008 to 2017, survival with more than three and four major morbidities decreased by more than 40% each.21 Nevertheless, persistent wide variation across NICUs indicates continued opportunities for improvement.

Another indicator for quality deficits in the neonatal intensive care setting is the widespread delivery of inappropriate care—defined as underuse, overuse, and misuse of interventions.22,23 Examples include the underuse of hand hygiene by NICU personnel,24 the overuse of antibiotics, and the misuse of medications because of medical errors. An example of an effort to address overuse and misuse is Choosing Wisely (www.choosingwisely.org).25 Choosing Wisely, an initiative of the American Board of Internal Medicine, has challenged national medical specialty societies to identify “Top Five” lists of commonly used tests or procedures whose necessity should be questioned and discussed with patients. The American Academy of Pediatrics Section of Neonatal Perinatal Medicine (SONPM) developed a Top Five list for neonatology using a multistep process that included expert input via a modified Delphi experiment and formal evidence assessment. To Err Is Human, the Institute of Medicine concluded that between 44,000 and 98,000 Americans die annually from hospital errors, which kill more Americans than breast cancer, traffic accidents, or AIDS.12

Preterm infants in the NICU are particularly vulnerable to medical errors, due to their small size, physiologic immaturity, and limited compensatory abilities.26,27 Medication errors are a common problem in sick newborns due to breakdowns in patient identification28 and rapid changes in physiologic maturity, body weight, and volumes of distribution that require frequent dose adjustments. NICU patients are more likely to experience a medication error than other hospital patients.29 For example, a study at two Boston hospitals documented that errors in the process of ordering, dispensing, or monitoring medications occurred for more than 90% of the infants cared for in the NICU.30 Another study, using a trigger tool in the electronic health record, identified 0.74 adverse events per patient,29 and 56% of these were deemed preventable. These estimates are likely only the tip of the iceberg.

Medication errors are not the only safety challenges preterm infants face. Using a voluntary, anonymous, errorreporting system established by the VON, Suresh et al.31 documented a broad range of errors at 54 NICUs. Only about half involved medications; the remainder involved a wide variety of errors, including errors in administration or method of using a treatment (14%), patient misidentification (11%), other system failure (9%), error or delay in diagnosis (7%), and error in the performance of an operation, procedure, or test (4%). A study from eight Dutch NICUs found that incidents concerning mechanical ventilation, blood products, intravascular lines, parenteral nutrition, and medication dosing errors posed the highest risk to patients in the NICU.32

The necessary engagement of parents or other primary caregivers for delivery of high-quality care for hospitalized children is now well recognized.33,34 Families contribute to the quality and safety of health care through their direct involvement in caregiving and decision-making for their loved ones and through engagement in clinical unit and hospital-wide quality and safety initiatives.35 In neonatal care, the critical role of parents as essential caregivers for preterm and ill newborns is undisputed. For example, active parent involvement in preterm infant caregiving leads to higher breastfeeding rates, earlier discharge, and improved long-term neurodevelopment.36 Conversely, untreated NICU-related parent stress and depression adversely affect preterm children’s long-term social, behavioral, and functional development.37 Despite strong evidence, family involvement in care and decision-making and in quality and safety initiatives is often lacking or variably implemented within and across neonatal care settings.

Furthermore, our health care delivery system has not reliably delivered many interventions that are deemed to be effective. McGlynn et al.38 in adults and later Mangione- Smith et al.39 in children demonstrated that Americans receive guideline-recommended care only about half the

time. In the newborn setting, antenatal steroids are an example where opportunities for improvement in administration have been documented.40–42

Finally, evidence is building that the care we provide and the outcomes of patients are not equitably distributed.43 Reasons for this include historical injustices, structural and institutional racism, social determinants of health, and uneven access to care. It is important to understand that these conditions can have downstream effects that span generations. Such inequities are not unique to the United States and are global issues. At the level of NICU care, important contributors to inequities include between-NICU and within-NICU variation in care.37,44 Between-NICU variation implies the segregation of groups into resource-limited hospitals.45 Within-NICU variation is the result of implicit or explicit bias by health care workers impacting the care of infants and their families.46 Quality improvement has been posited as a tool to address inequities but may improve, perpetuate, or even worsen them. Therefore all such activities should explicitly consider equity implications.

The inability of the health care system to consistently deliver the highest quality of care results in an enormous waste of human lives and valuable resources. Berwick and Hackbarth47 showed that “in just six categories of waste— overtreatment, failures of care coordination, failures in execution of care processes, administrative complexity, pricing failures, and fraud and abuse—the sum of the lowest available estimates exceeds 20% of total health care expenditures.” In the United States, the care of preterm infants consumes about $40 billion annually (adjusted for inflation).11 These health expenditures directly affect families and patients. More than half a million households file for bankruptcy annually due to medical reasons.48

With the recognition of the extent of the opportunities for improvement have come efforts by providers, provider networks, payers, nongovernmental organizations, and governmental agencies to improve the value of health care expenditures. At the provider network and health system level, efforts have focused on quality assurance and alignment of financial incentives. For example, quality assurance has been pursued through benchmarking of performance by provider networks such as the CPQCC49 or the VON,50 as well as other statewide neonatal collaborative organizations, including those in Massachusetts, Ohio, Tennessee, North Carolina, Florida, Wisconsin, and Michigan. Significant efforts have been made to develop, evaluate, and harmonize quality measures through organizations such as the National Quality Forum. Public reporting51–53 of quality measures and realignment of financial incentives away from a fee-forservice system toward a pay-for-quality system are becoming more widespread.54,55 We provide more detail on these issues later in this chapter.

At the hospital level, health care professionals have engaged in quality improvement efforts to meet the expectations of this changing marketplace. Several institutions have undergone fundamental reengineering of their health care delivery systems. This transformation has been the consequence of a

growing recognition that the current model of health care delivery is in many ways outmoded and has failed to incorporate many of the lessons that propelled other sectors of the economy to large gains in quality and productivity. When health care professionals work together in teams and include families, they can harness the joint wisdom of their respective expertise. Crowdsourcing of expertise is important because the expert health care professional mind is fallible and divergent,56 prone to systematic bias,57–59 limited in its ability to process complex information (maximally 7 ± 2 simultaneous variables),60 and unable to stay current on medical knowledge (doubling time of 8 years).22,61 Thus a hierarchical physician-centric approach to care results in clinical variability that is driven by provider rather than patient factors, and this ultimately degrades care. For all of these reasons, a new approach must be pursued that is based on flat team hierarchies, standardization and systematic use of clinical data, and evidence. Other industries have grappled with similar transitions and have provided a blueprint for improvement that is now actively pursued by many high-performing health systems. We briefly examine some of the historical foundations of these developments.

Shewhart framed the problem of process variation in terms of assignable and chance variation (Deming later referred to these as special and common cause variation) and developed the “control chart” to operationalize these concepts. Kiichiro Toyoda, the founder of Toyota Motor Company, was among Deming’s audience in 1950. Based on Deming’s lectures, Toyoda developed the Toyota Production System (TPS), an integrated sociotechnical system comprised of its management philosophy and practices. The system is a precursor of the more generic lean manufacturing. At the core of TPS is elimination of waste (muda) and total focus on reliable, high quality through continuous improvement (kaizen). Lean production focuses on just-in-time use of materials and optimization of production flow (mura) in response to customer demand. Toyota’s success derived from its innovative production engineering, which puts quality control in the hands of the frontline workers who can stop the production line and call for help when something goes wrong. However, production engineering is only part of story. An equally important contributor to Toyota’s success has been coined the “Toyota Way,” principles that embody a relentless focus on the needs and desires of customers.

Another variant of statistical process control originally developed by Motorola in 1985 is Six Sigma, which is a set of process improvement tools and strategies that seek to improve quality by minimizing defects and variability. In Six Sigma, a defect is defined as any process output that does not meet customer specifications or that could lead to creating an output that does not meet customer specifications. The term “Six Sigma process” means that, if one has 6 standard deviations between the process mean and the nearest specification limit, then only 3.4 out of 1 million outputs will fail to meet specifications. To illustrate, a 1-sigma process produces 69%, a 2-sigma process produces 31%, and a 6-sigma process produces 0.00034% defective outputs. Current evidence suggests that much of health care operates at the 1- or 2-sigma level.38,39 Six Sigma includes methodologies that lean on Deming’s PDSA cycle, such as DMAIC for reengineering existing processes: Define the problem. Measure key aspects. Analyze data for key relationships. Improve the current process (using a set of specific techniques). Control the future process.

Industrial quality improvement methods have been applied to health care for more than 30 years. In Curing Health Care: New Strategies for Quality Improvement, Berwick and co-workers64 provided the initial evidence that the tools of modern quality improvement, with which other industries have achieved breakthroughs in performance, can help in health care as well. Since then, the core ideas of quality improvement have been adapted to the needs of health care and implemented by health care organizations around the world. In neonatology, multidisciplinary teams from NICUs across the United States have applied quality improvement tools and methods to address the quality

and safety of medical care for newborn infants and their families.49,65–69

Successful adaptation of these industrial methods to the NICU setting requires recognition that health care is a process and that quality improvement requires process management. However, a focus on care delivery processes is not sufficient. Rather, process management needs to be accompanied by efforts to optimize the organizational environment in ways that promote continual learning, teamwork, and adherence to the mission of serving patients and families. Several theoretical frameworks exist to further our understanding of the complex interplay among the organizational environment, process, and outcomes.

### Definitions and Conceptual Frameworks for Quality

### Definitions

The Institute of Medicine defines quality-of-care delivery as the extent to which health services provided to individuals and patient populations improve desired health outcomes. In Crossing the Quality Chasm,13 it developed the following six domains of quality of delivery of care:

### Conceptual Frameworks for Quality

Various authors have developed conceptual or explanatory models that provide users with a systematic understanding of the components and facilitators of high-quality health care delivery. These frameworks are often adapted from other sciences, including manufacturing, engineering, organizational theory, and psychology. They share a common premise that high quality-of-care delivery is the result of efforts to create a work environment in which people work together to reliably execute processes that are known to work or thought to work, and to avoid care that is known to be unhelpful. We mention a few commonly applied ones here.

### Donabedian: Structure, Processes, Outcomes

Maybe the best-known framework for quality improvement was developed by Avedis Donabedian (1919–2000). Donabedian was the first to collate evidence on the quality of health care delivery; he developed a simple structure, process, and outcome model of quality that has been the mainstay of quality research.71 Structure traditionally has been interpreted as the physical facilities and the training and education of staff. This notion has been expanded to include executive leadership, organizational culture, organizational design, incentive structure, and information systems and technology.72 Processes include the activities that clinicians and support staff undertake as part of or in support of care delivery. Outcomes are the results of these activities.

### Pawson and Tilley: Realistic Evaluation

Realistic evaluation goes beyond the traditional cause-andeffect paradigm and poses that program effectiveness also depends on many interrelated contextual variables.73 This concept is formalized by the following formula: Context + Mechanism = Outcome, or C + M = O. A given intervention does not necessarily work for the group, but may be quite useful to some individuals, depending on their specific circumstances.

### Translation of Frameworks into Action

Frontline application of quality improvement efforts draws from the various theoretical frameworks presented in the preceding section. In the following, we highlight a few approaches that are commonly applied in neonatology. For practitioners, the practical application of quality improvement work may be more effective if supported by a formal approach, such as those provided by the Model for Improvement, Deming’s integrated management model, lean manufacturing, and Six Sigma. Each of these models makes use of various management tools to assist with project planning and execution, including team organization, process mapping, problem identification, problem resolution, task prioritization, and execution. The first question, which refers to the aim(s) of the project, is “What are we trying to accomplish?” In other words, what are the main outcomes that the team is trying to change or improve. There are several considerations when considering the aim of a prospective project. Are there historical and/or peer benchmark data to suggest that there is a problem in the local NICU? Is there a variation in outcomes among NICUs to suggest that variations in practices can lead to differences in outcomes? Are there evidence-based practices that have been shown to improve the outcome of interest? The aim should be well defined and realistic. It should also be quantifiable and encompass a defined time period.77

One helpful technique for crafting a well-designed aim statement is the SMART Aim approach (Table 5.1). An example of a suboptimal aim statement may be, “Reduce infection rates in our NICU.” Although a worthy goal, that statement is vague, and it will be difficult to assess if it has been accomplished. In contrast, the following aim statement is more specific and follows the SMART Aim approach: “Reduce by 50% the number of central lineassociated bloodstream infections as defined by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention in infants born at 24 to 30 weeks’ gestational age in the NICU by using an insertion and maintenance bundle over a 12-month time period.”

A process measure related to reduction of nosocomial infection could be the use of an insertion checklist. The third question, which corresponds to the strategic changes that will lead to improvement, is “What changes can we make that will result in improvement?” These changes should be evidence based but must also account for local cultural and organizational characteristics. ### Integrated Quality Management

Another model for quality improvement in health care focuses on the elimination of process variation. This approach derives from Deming’s work and has been adapted with success for health care by several organizations. For example, Intermountain Healthcare helped refocus the health system’s quality improvement efforts on process standardization, active management, and ongoing organizational learning. Work units identify high-value clinical processes and set up multidisciplinary provider teams to develop evidence-based, detailed care pathways (shared baselines) to execute these processes in a consistent, reliable manner. Use of the term shared baseline, rather than protocol or guideline, highlights its development and ongoing refinement by the local work unit. Control rests with frontline care providers, not managers. The purpose of these shared baselines is to minimize the variation that is driven by providers and not accounted for by patient need. Providers are encouraged to deviate from the shared baseline as warranted by the needs of individual patients. This approach allows providers to bring their expertise to bear on the clinical situations that truly warrant an alternative treatment approach. It also effectively ameliorates concerns regarding “cookbook” medicine, given an explicit expectation that no guideline perfectly fits any patient. On the contrary, providers are asked to assess situations where they have deviated from the shared baseline. This information is then used to modify the shared baseline when appropriate. Overall, the use of shared baselines makes the workflow more predictable, efficient, safer for patients, and less stressful for nursing staff.80–83

Similar to the Model for Improvement, the modified Deming approach uses key measures to track cornerstones of the process, clinical outcomes, service outcomes (such as patient satisfaction), and cost. The key to promote organizational learning and standardization is tracking process deviations and systems to modify the shared baselines according to ongoing provider input. To facilitate the success of local projects, strategic staff from clinical work units receive training and technical support for their improvement work from a centralized quality improvement institute. Institutional commitment is foundational to achieving excellence in care delivery.81

### Lean

Lean is a quality improvement model that focuses on reducing process waste and improving process flow.84 The seven forms of waste in Lean are overproduction, inventory, waiting, transportation, defects, staff movement, and unnecessary processing. Lean focuses on customers, patients, and families and encourages users to examine their process steps and eliminate those that do not provide value to customers. Attention to “flow” attempts to optimize the use of time and resources.

Although meaningful in their differences, their common thread lies in the centrality of managing work processes while optimizing the care delivery environment. Organizational goals for high-quality care delivery and patient safety are undergirded by strategies that encourage continuous monitoring of processes and outcomes, facilitate continuous learning, and minimize unwarranted waste and process variation.

Not everything that can be counted counts. Not everything that counts can be counted.

It is essential that quality improvement be data driven. First, to identify opportunities for quality improvement, data are necessary to determine performance relative to historical performance or peer-derived benchmarks. Second, essential to the Plan-Do-Study-Act approach, when quality improvement has been undertaken, data are used to assess the extent to which each specific intervention has or has not been effective, as well as to identify unintended negative consequences. Although we use the term data, what we are really collecting is information that we can use to guide the course of our quality improvement. To lower the burden of collection, we should only select the minimal amount of data that can guide our progress, and, if possible, we should use data that can be derived from or efficiently incorporated into the workflow.

### Data Elements for Assessment

In following Donabedian’s framework, the main data types can be categorized as structural or contextual measures, process measures, and outcome measures (including medical outcomes, cost, and service outcomes such as family satisfaction with care). The three most important considerations for any data element are its clarity of definition, the ease with which it can be identified and extracted from the medical record or its administrative source, and its universality across time and location. Stable definitions across time are important to assess the extent to which a morbidity has decreased or increased over time. Agreed-upon definitions are essential to compare outcomes across NICUs both within and across countries. The widespread adoption of the definitions developed by the VON has made both national and international comparisons possible.

### Structure and Institutional Context

Although the focus of quality improvement has largely been on changing processes of care, there is an increasing awareness of the importance of the context in which improvement activities occur. In addition to changing the behavior

of members of the health care team, quality improvement often relies on changing systems of care and with it the organizational environment. Efforts at measuring care structure and context have been motivated by numerous studies linking these aspects to clinical and organizational outcomes.

Maybe the best-examined aspect of structure is the patient-to-nurse ratio. In adult and pediatric intensive care unit settings,85,86 rising patient-to-nurse ratios have been linked to worse clinical outcomes and higher mortality. In neonatology, this link is less well understood.87 Although most studies imply a benefit of greater staffing ratios, this finding is not uniform.88 Nurse staffing ratios may be suboptimal in general and associated with nosocomial infection rates.89 Other examples of structural measures that can impact the quality of care include academic teaching status, 24-hour coverage by an in-house intensivist, patient volume, and level of care.

Contextual measures are different from traditional measures in that they attempt to assess the links between how care delivery is organized and supported by the institution and quality of care. Kaplan and colleagues90 developed a framework for examining these linkages and coined it Model for Understanding Success in Quality (MUSIQ). This model includes different components that support improvements in the processes and outcomes of care delivery at various levels of the institution, including characteristics of the improvement team (diversity, physician involvement, leadership) and the NICU, as well as higher-level organizational functions (quality improvement leadership and administrative support).91

One well-researched aspect of care context is safety culture. The NICU safety culture describes the shared norms of leadership, staff, and the organization with regard to patient safety, or, simply, the “way we do things around here.” Culture influences quality of care in important ways. Whether a process is failure proof depends on how reliably it is implemented. Non-adherence to care standards may reflect a clinical need, but also lack of a standard or willful disregard. Safety culture assessments attempt to capture safety context through surveys that ask about health care worker perceptions. The measured domains are referred to as climates (e.g., teamwork climate, safety climate). The term “climate” reflects that perceptions are shared among health workers; that is, they cluster more strongly within a work unit (e.g., the NICU) than between work units.

Ongoing assessment of safety culture is a requirement for hospital accreditation by the Joint Commission, an accreditation agency that assesses safety and quality of care. There are several validated survey tools to measure safety culture.26,92–94 The Safety Attitudes Questionnaire (SAQ) reflects caregiver attitudes on safety culture, is psychometrically sound, and has demonstrated wide interinstitutional variation across NICUs.95 Safety culture, as measured by the SAQ, has been linked to a variety of clinical and organizational outcomes. Most famously, this instrument was used by Pronovost and co-workers96 in their implementation of the Comprehensive Unit-based Safety Program


## PDF page 93

(CUSP) in 103 adult ICUs in Michigan, where, through implementation of a checklist and a focus on teamwork and safety, median catheter-related bloodstream infection rates were reduced to zero. This program has been endorsed for implementation in all ICUs by the US government. In a study of 44 NICUs and over 2000 respondents, teamwork was negatively correlated with health care–associated infections among VLBW infants.97 Of note, physicians tend to rate safety culture significantly higher than do nurses and other personnel, highlighting an apparent disconnect in perceptions.

Various approaches to improving safety culture have been described. Among them are leadership engagement via Leadership WalkRounds98 and a teamwork training curriculum developed by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, called Team Strategies and Tools to Enhance Performance and Patient Safety (TeamSTEPPS). The TeamSTEPPS intervention includes assessment, training, and sustainment phases focused on four core competencies: (1) team leadership, (2) situation monitoring, (3) mutual support, and (4) communication.99 This approach has been used in multiple health care settings,100,101 including one reported intervention that included NICU providers and demonstrated an improvement in perceptions of teamwork.102

Another emerging aspect of care context is health care worker resilience, operationally often defined as the absence of burnout. Burnout describes a condition of fatigue, detachment, and cynicism resulting from prolonged high levels of stress.103 In the critical care setting, burnout rates may be consequent to high workload, frequent changes in technology and guidelines, and the emotional challenges of dealing with critically ill patients and their families. Severe burnout affects between a quarter and half of health care workers,103,104 and the COVID-19 pandemic has resulted in a substantial increase.105 Burnout has negative consequences for health care providers, organizations, and patient care (Box 5.3).

A systematic review identified that both organizational as well as individually focused interventions are effective in reducing physician burnout.106 A randomized controlled trial of an individual intervention program (WISER) using positive psychology tools delivered via cell phone to NICU health care workers demonstrated lasting benefits with regard to burnout, work-life balance, and depression among participants.107 Overall, context provides the soil in which quality improvement thrives or withers. Leadership recognition of these factors is critical; to provide high quality of care to our patients, we must also care for ourselves and our colleagues.

### Balancing Measures

When teams work on improving one aspect of care, it is possible that related outcomes suffer; for example, a decrease in growth velocity may be observed with a focus on improving breast milk feeding rates. Balancing measures may include clinical, service, or cost outcomes. These balancing measures should be collected in or close to real time to detect any unintended consequences of quality improvement projects. Although traditionally balancing measures were developed to assess potential adverse effects of improvement activities, they can also show that the new way of doing things is safe.

### Risk Adjustment/Fair Comparisons

For performance assessment within an organization whose patient demographics are stable over time, tracking a process or outcome measure over time may be sufficient to

spur quality improvement activities and assess their impact. However, for comparative performance assessments between organizations, as currently applied for purposes such as setting benchmarks, pay-for-performance, or public reporting, further statistical and methodological considerations are required to ensure fair comparisons.

The use of risk adjustment levels the playing field by accounting for expected rates of positive or negative outcomes based on the risk profiles (case mix) seen at each institution. Expectation is pivotal to the notion of quality, which can be operationally defined as the degree to which the observed rate of an outcome or process is higher, lower, or equal to its expected rate.23 An observed rate of morbidity that is higher than the expected rate indicates that there is an opportunity for quality improvement. Does hospital A with an observed neonatal mortality rate (NMR) of 5 provide better care than hospital B with an observed NMR of 10? Without knowing the expected rate of mortality at these two hospitals, it is impossible to assess their relative quality of care. For example, hospital B might be a tertiary care center, in which an observed NMR of 10 is much lower than its expected NMR of 18. Hospital A could be a primary care hospital, in which an observed NMR of 5 is much higher than its expected NMR of 3.

Mathematically, risk-adjustment methods level the playing field using one of the variants of multivariate regression. Typically based on data on risks and outcomes from multiple institutions, an estimate of the impact of each of the risk factors considered to be important to the outcome is used to create an expectation model. The model can then generate the expected outcome of a NICU based on its specific case mix.

The factors used in risk adjustment for neonatal quality assessment will encompass both maternal sociodemographic and clinical factors, as well as infant factors. Two essential requirements guide variable selection: (1) the variable should be a risk factor for the outcome of interest, and (2) the variable should not be under the control of the entity being evaluated. Mother’s age, education, payer for delivery, and perinatal complications, as well as the infant’s birth weight, gestational age, plurality, intrauterine growth, birth defects, and gender, are risk factors that are commonly used as risk adjusters to control for institutional differences in case mix so as to make fair comparisons across NICUs.23,91 They are highly predictive of morbidity and mortality and are not under the control of clinicians in the NICU. Using this approach to quality assessment, the ratio of the observed number of deaths (or adverse outcomes) to the expected number of deaths (or adverse outcomes), the standardized mortality (or morbidity) ratio (SMR), can then be calculated.

In addition to reporting the SMR as a measure of performance, an alternative approach is to calculate the difference between the observed and expected number of cases (O– E), where the expected number can be determined using the same regression approach described earlier. ### Data Sources Administrative Data

In addition to data collected specifically for the purpose of quality improvement, the use of currently available data such as hospital administrative data and vital records data may allow for increased data capture and efficiency. We refer to this repurposed data as green data. Secondary datasets that have been used to evaluate quality of care include the uniform hospital discharge database (which captures diagnostic, procedural, and costs data), birth certificate files, and death certificates files. Although these administrative databases were not primarily designed for evaluating perinatal care, they contain data elements that have made it possible to examine risk-adjusted perinatal complication rates,132 maternal morbidities,133 cesarean delivery,134 and neonatal readmission rates.135 However, secondary data sources do have several disadvantages. They may not have all of the desired data items, and definitions might not be the same as those used in NICU databases. Underreporting may be significant.136 For example, congenital anomalies are markedly underreported on both birth and death certificates.137

Because both clinical and administrative databases vary with respect to the extent of their maternal, neonatal, morbidity, and mortality, as well as important sociodemographic data, the linkage of multiple datasets is a strategy to obtain a more comprehensive picture of perinatal risks and outcomes at the regional, community, and hospital level.136,138 Examples of population-based studies using a linked database include the relationship between discharge timing after birth and infant readmission,135 risk factors and neonatal outcomes,132 neonatal outcomes in childbearing beyond the age of 40,139 factors associated with the birth of infants with very low birth weight at non-NICU hospitals,140 trends in cesarean delivery for various conditions,141,142 and quality assessment of perinatal regionalization.143

The use of secondary data can also provide a highly efficient approach to building a complete quality improvement reporting system. The California Maternal Quality Care Collaborative (CMQCC, www.cmqcc.org) provides hospitals close to real-time (within 45 days from discharge) data based on linking the infant’s birth certificate to the mother’s and the infant’s discharge ICD-10 codes. In addition to perinatal quality metrics, the CMQCC also provides a near real-time assessment of data quality as part of an ongoing initiative to improve its administrative data sources. The reports also contain confidential drill-down analyses in order to identify ways to improve performance. For example, if there was an increase in early-term elective delivery for a hospital (Fig. 5.4), then one could examine cases over a period of time to check if there are certain diagnoses that may be contributing to an increase in the elective delivery rate. The rate of elective delivery can also be calculated by a provider to see if there are opportunities to work with individuals who may be contributing most to an increased rate. A demonstration site with artificial data is available at https ://demo.datacenter.cmqcc.org.

### Clinical Data

When data are collected primarily for the purpose of quality assessment, computer-based data entry systems using local or web-based data entry interfaces allow for efficiency in checking for errors such as missing fields or out-of-range entries. An advantage of these systems is that they detect errors in completeness, range, and consistency as the data are being abstracted and entered. The result is a clean record at the point of data entry that avoids costly offsite error detection and correction cycles.144

However, in carrying out a quality improvement initiative, it is often necessary to collect your own primary process, balancing, and outcome measures, as well as patient characteristics (if risk adjustment is needed). Data collection is always time consuming, so it is important to collect the minimal number of data elements needed to assess whether or not interventions are working. Incorporating data collection into the workflow can also lessen the burden. Data elements should be clearly and systematically defined, using universal or published definitions to enable comparison with other hospitals working on the same quality issue or published results.

Obtaining data from the electronic health record is an extremely efficient technique; however, it is crucial that the definitions that your hospital use in the electronic record are standardized across everyone who enters data and are based on widely recognized criteria. Unless all entities use the same definition, an objective and consistent assessment within and across NICUs may not be possible from electronic medical record data. For example, if the diagnosis of patent ductus arteriosus differs across individual NICUs, then comparisons of the incidence or the effects of treatment will be meaningless.

### Data for Selection

A rising demand exists for transparency and public reporting of quality in health care with the goal to spur consumer choice, allow for performance-based reimbursement, and motivate better care. In other areas of medicine, public release of information on quality has been on the rise (http:// www.cms.gov/Medicare/Quality-Initiatives-Patient-Assessment-Instruments/HospitalQualityInits/OutcomeMeasure s.html), and this can be expected in neonatology as well.

U.S. News & World Report releases annual rankings of children’s hospitals and subspecialties, including neonatology. The ranking methodology is based on Donabedian’s structure, process, outcomes model, with points assigned for meeting each criterion (https://health.usnews.com/besthospitals/pediatric-rankings/neonatal-care). Expert opinion traditionally also represented an additional component, but its influence has decreased with the advent of quantitative data. Little is known regarding the validity of these rankings. Many variables have only face validity to support their use. Nevertheless, these rankings are important to hospital administrators who use them for advertising. The focus on optimizing these rankings may have positive and negative effects. Positive aspects include hospitals dedicating resources to support care structure and quality improvement activities based on measured priorities. Negative aspects include a potential misalignment between hospital leadership focus and NICU priorities. Furthermore, one can expect that hospitals will game their data given the reputational value.

The Leapfrog Group (http://www.leapfroggroup.org/), a consortium of Fortune 500 companies, provides hospitalspecific performance data to the public. Although measurement of perinatal care is limited, the group does provide a rating of quality of care for high-risk deliveries, defined as the care provided for VLBW infants. Neonatal teams must understand the strengths and weaknesses of different methods for making risk-adjusted comparisons of neonatal outcomes as they attempt to assist the public in understanding these data and to use the data themselves to monitor, evaluate, and improve the quality of care that they provide.

One of the rationales for public reporting of health care quality is the concept that the existence of such reports motivates and influences providers and organizations to improve their quality of care. The Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (http://www.talkingquality.ahrq.gov/about. htm) has promoted the use of public reporting and guided its implementation for many organizations. Although there needs to be continued research on the impact of public reporting on health care quality, thus far results are mixed. Public reports do not appear to inform consumer choice significantly, and improvements in quality measures are moderate at best.151 Consumer Reports (www.consumerre ports.org) is another consumer-oriented organization that presents hospital quality assessments.

These sources have not been well validated and have shown results that conflict with other measurement tools.152 These publications generally use league tables (rank ordering of institutions) and can provide an idea of how one

center might compare with other similar centers for specific measures. However, the interpretation of league tables must be informed by the context of reliability of measurements, adequate risk adjustment, and consistency of results, as what may appear to be an orderly ranking may not ultimately denote significant differences among NICUs.153 For example, in a league table, the difference between a top and middle performer may not be clinically or statistically significant. Nevertheless, hospitals feature such ratings prominently in marketing their services, and substantial intramural efforts are devoted to maximizing ratings.

The use of public reporting for perinatal care is relatively underdeveloped; however, it may be an untapped strategy for improving quality. Women of childbearing age are among the most frequent users of the Internet, and pregnancy is the featured topic of many websites geared toward this group.154–157 Studies have shown that women in general, and specifically expectant and current mothers, view the Internet as a way to connect to other women having similar experiences and to share information with one another,158–163 so that each woman who finds a public reporting website useful may communicate that experience to others. Some providers may feel that the development of well-designed public reporting websites that provide quality data on maternal and neonatal measures ensures their consumer relevance, accuracy, and fairness, and some rating websites are beginning to include these measures already in their hospital portals. For example, Yelp is now showing several perinatal indicators as a standard feature (Fig. 5.6).

### National Quality Measures

Interest in quality assessment continues to increase for perinatal medicine. An important development in ensuring that proposed measures can actually reflect the quality of care has been the work of the National Quality Forum (NQF; www.qualityforum.org), a multi-stakeholder group that evaluates proposed quality measures. In considering measures, several important areas are assessed, including the importance of the measure to making significant gains in health care quality, the reliability and validity of the measure, the extent to which the results can be understood and be useful for decision making, and the feasibility of collection. The Joint Commission published a list of core perinatal measures in 2007. A list of current measures from the NQF is noted in Table 5.4. When considering a potential measure for widespread use, it is important to have evidence that the aspect of care that the measure reflects has been shown to be malleable—that is, shown to be improvable in at least one multi-institutional quality improvement initiative.

As the list of quality measures continues to grow, it may become overwhelming for stakeholders, including clinicians, payers, and consumers, to extract a meaningful global picture of quality-of-care delivery at a given NICU. In addition, Profit demonstrated that correlations between quality measures of the care provided to VLBW infants are generally weak, meaning that it is difficult to infer overall qualityof-care delivery based on a small subset of clinical measures. Strategies for global assessments of quality may provide a more comprehensive assessment.164 We explore two of these in the next section.

### Data for Improvement

Although we have so far discussed the collection and presentation of data for provider selection, the active work of quality improvement requires different considerations. First, as the purpose of data collection for quality improvement is geared toward identifying potential areas of need and tracking change as soon as possible, there may be a lower threshold for identifying what might be considered an outlying value. In other words, data that are “good enough” to expose areas sorely in need for improvement may be better than waiting for “perfect” data. Second, there may be additional demands for data collection. Although some of the data sources noted in the preceding may allow for facilitation of quality improvement activities to some extent, there are several other aspects of data necessary for active quality improvement or data collections that may allow a specific question in quality improvement to be answered.

When data are analyzed for the purposes of benchmarking and comparisons, historical data that are collected over a stable period of time may be optimal. However, amid an effort to improve quality for specific processes or outcomes, data will need to be collected and presented in or close to real time.

A strategic aspect of data collection for quality improvement may be the need for additional process measures to be collected. For example, in an effort to increase temperature

of very preterm infants at delivery, several important variables could contribute to this effort, including delivery room temperature, use of devices such as plastic wrap or a chemical warming mattress, and use of a checklist or noting of adequate team communication before delivery. Another example could be a project to reduce central line infections, in which the team may want to collect data on whether a line insertion or maintenance checklist is being followed, number of line days, and whether the line necessity is being assessed regularly. Although temperature and the presence of an infection may already be part of real-time data collection, the other data elements may not be typically collected or even noted in the medical record in a systematic fashion. During the quality improvement project, the additional data may be collected to facilitate the quality improvement cycle and identify areas for improvement.

When several groups participate together in a collaborative to improve quality, a database program may serve as a common data repository and serve several useful purposes. First, it allows for the systematic collection of data in real time and for the specific purpose of the quality improvement project. Second, it allows each center to assess how it is doing compared with other groups and therefore to identify areas for improvement. Third, it can give insight into what processes may be most important to the main outcome of interest for the quality improvement project.

An essential purpose of data assessment of provider performance is to serve as an agent for change. To fulfill this purpose, data must be available in a timely manner. Data that are too old cannot inform decision-making reliably. To meet this need, data reports have been evolving from fixed, periodic (e.g., annual) reports to more flexible reporting that also allows for user choice in measures presented, time period, comparison groups, and type of presentation. Both the VON and the CPQCC provide users with secure real-time access to customizable data queries that allow for monitoring of trends over time and benchmarking against peer groups. Although the user can take advantage of the multiple features for selection of data presentation, there is also the option of monitoring performance across multiple measures on interactive dashboards (Fig. 5.7A–F). This dashboard shows case mix–adjusted individual NICU performance in comparison to its peers. CPQCC now also displays a health equity dashboard that allows users to view performance on multiple equity-sensitive process and outcome measures by race/ethnicity (Fig. 5.7F). Each metric is shown with regard to the performance difference between the best and worst performing subgroups.

Run charts and standardized process control (SPC) charts (Fig. 5.7E) are powerful tools for quality improvement.165 A run chart displays a series of data according to a criterion, usually time. Time is recorded on the x-axis, and the quality indicator is shown on the y-axis. This simple run chart can provide important insights regarding trends and variation in the process that results in the quality indicator. ### Global Assessments of Perinatal Quality: The Baby-MONITOR

Another method to cut through the thicket of individual quality measures is to combine them meaningfully into a composite indicator. To simplify the presentation of quality assessment, a composite quality rating may ultimately serve as a simple way to quickly assess quality. Composite indicators of quality are aggregates of individual measures of quality. Numerous choices have to be made by developers with regard to measure selection, definition, weighting, and aggregation phases. These choices require that development of composite indicators proceeds in an explicit, systematic, and transparent manner, and that the impact of the developers’ choices on NICU performance ratings is subjected to sensitivity analysis.

The Baby-MONITOR is an attempt at providing such a simplified global assessment tool. The composite is an aggregate of nine measures of quality routinely collected by members of the VON and the CPQCC. The nine measures were selected by an expert panel and validated by a sample of clinicians.166,167 Quality measures are risk adjusted, standardized by calculating an observed minus expected event difference, and equally weighted and averaged. The measure has been robust to sensitivity analyses invoking different aggregation and weighting schemes. Subsequent use has shown large variation across California NICUs by level of care168 and by race/ethnicity.43 National use of the Baby- MONITOR in VON data similarly revealed disparities,

Although composite measurement is still in its early days, such tools may prove useful in a variety of ways. They may allow users to monitor overall progress on key quality measures and to summarily assess the effect of quality interventions on performance. Through a more holistic focus, composite measurement may provide an incentive for NICUs to engage in systems-based quality improvement efforts that have the potential to affect multiple aspects of quality simultaneously. In addition, it may facilitate discussions with other stakeholder groups on quality of care. Without a comprehensive quality assessment tool, external stakeholders may increasingly subject NICUs to piecemeal appraisals that may not do justice to their overall performance.

### Quality and Safety Applied

The previous sections have focused on the history, theoretical frameworks, models, and data necessities that underpin quality improvement work, at either the individual unit or the health system level. Neonatology has been at the vanguard of the quality improvement field. Experimentation and refinement of applied methods occur in a large variety of formats. Neonatal providers undertake improvement work at the individual unit level and in collaboration with

vast networks. Formats include in-person or web-based collaboration, and methods for improvement use quantitative as well as qualitative techniques. For improvement work to be effective, a few principles deserve explicit recognition.

### Interdisciplinary Collaboration

NICU care is rarely the sole purview of any one discipline, and most care is complex, requiring a multidisciplinary approach. Nursing, the therapies (respiratory therapy, physical therapy, occupational therapy, speech and language therapy), pharmacy, and social work are the most common disciplines in addition to medicine that provide direct care to neonates. Nursing provides the majority of direct care to infants, so their involvement in quality improvement is essential. Efforts to address common quality improvement problems in neonatal care such as thermoregulation, unplanned extubation, nosocomial infection, medication errors, or skin breakdown are considered nurse-sensitive indicators of quality; thus neonatal quality improvement work should include partnerships with nursing to identify the problem, gather data, and trial solutions.171

### Families as Quality Improvement Partners

Another key partnership in quality improvement work that is not explicit in the frameworks and models described above involves the parents/guardians and other primary caregivers. Not only do families contribute to the quality and safety of their individual infants by contributing key observations and participating in daily care, but they can also contribute at a unit and institutional level to the quality improvement program, providing the perspective of “lived experience,” along with the patients. The Agency for Healthcare Quality (AHRQ; https://www.ahrq.gov/patientsafety/patients-families/engagingfamilies/index.html) published evidence-based guidelines and a toolkit to enable patients, families, and health professionals to work together as partners to promote improvements in care. Over the past decades, the NICU has become a leader in engaging families in improvement work (Box 5.4).172

In addition, the most valuable neonatal quality improvement endeavor that a hospital can embark on is the implementation or expansion of family-centered care (FCC) initiatives. In the NICU, parents should be regarded as essential care providers and significant members of their baby’s medical team. Because of the symbiotic nature of the parent-child relationship, parental involvement has been shown to improve overall health outcomes for both parents and babies, a priority and natural outcome of successful FCC. Moreover, FCC has been proven to facilitate bonding and cognitive development and to positively impact the mental health of the mother-baby dyad. When adopted consistently, FCC can empower and encourage patient families on their NICU journeys.173

### Quality Improvement as a Tool to Address Health Disparities

Despite striving for improvement for all patients, unconscious bias and structural racism permeate all aspects of society, including our health care system; thus quality improvement initiatives have the potential to perpetuate or widen health disparities.177 However, with an explicit focus on improving outcomes for groups experiencing a disparity, quality improvement initiatives may reduce these disparities. Equity-focused quality improvement offers an actionoriented, inclusive framework whereby equity is integrated throughout the design and implementation of a quality improvement initiative, from the development of a SMART Aim approach to designing and testing change ideas.178

Stratifying local data by sociodemographic variables, such as race, ethnicity, preferred language, or insurance status, is a critical first step to identify a disparity. When interpreting stratified data, teams explicitly discuss what the specific sociodemographic variable represents. For example, if data are stratified by race/ethnicity, discussions should focus on how race is a social construct and a proxy for racism and social determinants of health. Comparator groups are then selected based on specific criteria, such as population size or performance on a specific metric. Data can be compared within a NICU or between NICUs using local, regional, or national data.

Stakeholder mapping is inclusive and centers around patients, families, and community voices. Eliciting the “voice of the customer” through informal interviews and surveys informs initiative design, planning, and implementation. Stakeholders brainstorm root causes, specifically examining modifiable systems and processes, including racism and bias in health care systems and policies. Interventions are selected, prioritized, designed, and tested, targeting specific barriers and needs of the groups experiencing disparities. Data visualization tools are adapted to display and monitor disparity trends over time. Results are made easily accessible and disseminated to all stakeholders, including patients and community stakeholders. A case example can be found in Box 5.6.

It has been noted that the time required for new knowledge generated by clinical trials to be incorporated into general practice may be 17 years.179 Therefore one could argue that the study of the translation of evidence into practice is a crucial subsequent step in maximizing and expediting the impact of the discoveries made in clinical trials.180 The study of quality improvement activities may sometimes not be amenable to a traditional trial, and such a trial may not be the optimal approach. Quality improvement research and implementation science share related aims and methods but are in some ways quite distinct. Both approaches aim to improve health care quality; however, quality improvement has usually focused on a specific problem of poor performance in relation to existing standards, addressing safety issues, or reducing adverse outcomes in a specific health care clinical setting. In contrast, implementation science has usually been focused more upstream to explore and explain underutilization of evidence-based practices or technologies or overutilization/misutilization of ineffective practices (e.g., de-implementation). Implementation science has an explicit aim of developing generalizable knowledge beyond the individual unit, institution, or system. Quality improvement research is often aimed at informing local or within-region or system practices. Implementation science and quality improvement research approaches are effective when applied synergistically. For example, quality improvement research may identify that there is underutilization of neonatal interventions. Implementation science research will explore the potential barriers and enablers to uptake and sustainment of the practices and lead to a series of further

studies to test interventions based on theories of behavior or systems change. When the research has demonstrated an effective implementation strategy, ongoing quality improvement monitoring will inform the sustained impact of the practices.181

The importance of publishing and therefore disseminating the results of quality improvement projects has been recognized by the pediatric community, and several journals publish such reports. Another recognition by the broader medical community of the importance of quality improvement research has been the formation of the Standards for Quality Improvement Reporting Excellence (SQUIRE) guidelines (Table 5.5). These guidelines and their 2.0 iteration were established to promote rigor in designing and reporting quality improvement research.182 Although the usual conventions for publishing scientific papers largely apply, the SQUIRE guidelines recognize several relatively unique aspects of quality improvement research. First, although a rigorous clinical trial requires prespecification of patient groups and interventions, the course of a quality improvement study may be somewhat dynamic. Improvement strategies may be modified in response to feedback from the ongoing project. The results of a clinical trial are “hidden” until the end of the study to avoid bias and change of behavior from the investigators. On the other hand, the clinicians involved in a quality improvement project may continuously try to learn from the contextual environment and ongoing results and subsequently modify their behavior to increase the impact of the intervention.

For the mentioned reasons, Berwick183 has referred to randomized controlled trials as being “an impoverished way to learn” in the context of quality improvement, suggesting that learning from experience “while doing” can be an important part of improving the quality of care. In a simulation study comparing “learning” and putting into practice findings from significant randomized trials only versus quality improvement collaborative projects, the quality improvement model was found to be a potentially superior approach.184

In addition to the aspect of learning while doing, a patient-level randomization study for quality improvement may not be feasible, as improvement activities on intervention subjects in a single NICU are likely to contaminate the control group. Approaches such as cluster randomization or stepped wedge designs may be better suited for experimental studies of quality improvement.185,186 The Canadian Neonatal Network conducted a cluster randomized trial of 12 NICUs in which half of the units focused on nosocomial infections and the other half focused on reducing bronchopulmonary dysplasia rates.187 Although bronchopulmonary dysplasia was reduced only in the corresponding group, nosocomial infections decreased in both groups. Their findings may suggest that quality improvement activities in one area may also influence quality in other areas, making the interpretation of such trials challenging. ### Quality and Safety in the International Context

Although this chapter has focused on some of the quality work done in the United States, similarly sophisticated regional and national collaborative networks, as well as quality efforts by individual NICUs, are underway throughout the world. The quality improvement aims in many high- and upper-middle-income countries are reductions in neonatal morbidities and improving the value and experience of care, such as those outlined in the United States. However, the quality improvement aims in many low- and lower-middle-income countries (LMICs) remain focused on reducing neonatal mortality rates. Although access to care, including delivery in a health facility, remains a challenge in many LMIC settings, access to care is no longer viewed as a priority competing with quality of care by those implementing newborn health programs globally.189 The international community is focused on improving care and inequities in health outcomes for infants and their families, by targeting Sustainable Development Goal 3.2 to end preventable deaths of newborns and to reduce the global neonatal mortality rate to 12 or fewer per 1000 live births in all countries by 2030.190 However, based on progress to date, it is predicted that many countries in Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa will not reach these goals without an enhanced focus on equity, quality of perinatal and essential newborn care, and improved institutional care for small or sick newborns and their families.191

The World Health Organization (WHO) recognizes their key position in establishing standards for improving the quality of maternal and newborn care in health facilities,192 the care for small and sick newborns in health facilities,193 communicating human resource strategies to improve newborn care in health facilities in LMICs,194 and recommending maternal and newborn care for a positive postnatal experience.195 The second edition of the WHO Essential Newborn Care Course is included in WHO resources that integrate quality improvement discussion points and reflection questions into WHO’s foundational materials for the essential care of every newborn used by health care providers worldwide (https://www.w ho.int/tools/essential-newborn-care-training-course).196 These standards and seminal documents are necessary for a culture change in neonatal care, embracing the notion that daily improvements in care are at the core of our work as health care professionals. However, in order to operationalize standards and identify and quantify gaps in care, we must also establish systems that allow interrogation of the “Know-Do gap,” the difference between providers’ knowledge of appropriate care and the care they actually provide in a clinical encounter.197,198 Without data, health care providers and the systems they work in are powerless to improve the care they provide, learn from their own practice, collaborate effectively with peer facilities, or advocate successfully for the resources they need.199,200

The power and excitement of collaborative quality improvement, key ingredients to maximize sustainability, often come from the development of community. Many neonatal units have found value in joining international neonatal networks or peer learning communities, such as VON. Recognizing that the trajectory to reach global neonatal improvement targets in resource-limited settings will be dependent on initiatives led by LMIC clinicians and experts, VON has proudly worked with Ethiopian leaders to co-develop quality improvement education and tools to serve as the foundation for the Ethiopian Neonatal Network.201 Building on these efforts and momentum, VON is partnering with the African Neonatal Association to develop an African peer learning community with a foundation of a co-designed data system and quality improvement strategy, harmonizing when possible and collaborating with

Although there are many barriers along the path to facility-level data in an LMIC setting, much progress has been made, and future projects can build upon these successes and lessons learned. Indeed, a systematic accounting of improvement efforts that work and those that do not is necessary to leverage and spread existing knowledge, contributing to the community’s understanding of potentially better practices and the diverse environments in which they are implemented.

### Conclusion

Neonatal teams have been at the forefront of adapting and evolving industrial quality improvement methods.203 These efforts have yielded great benefits to infants requiring neonatal intensive care and their families. Quality and safety are emerging fields, and the supporting science is being developed. At its core, quality improvement endeavors are founded in experiential learning, but as rigor in methods advances, so does generalizability. Despite its relative youth, it is quality improvement that may hold the greatest hope for solving one of the most important issues of our time: health equity. We think the field will make a meaningful contribution in this area. Indeed, although more work needs to be done, the indication is that quality improvement work is, in fact, health equity work, as over the past decade disparity gaps have narrowed in many areas.204–207
